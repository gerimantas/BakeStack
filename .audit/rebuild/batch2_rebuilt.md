## Tip B2-03: Determining the Doneness of Honey Cake Layers

**Body:**

Delicate and seemingly innocuous, honey cake layers demand attention in the baking process, much like any other type of cakes.

When preparing honey cake layers, there are two potential, yet undesirable mistakes:

▪️ Underbaking them
▪️ Overbaking them

Determining the doneness of your cakes is simple — just observe their appearance! Let's explore cakes made from three types of honey dough as an illustration (for a refresher on honey dough types, refer to the post from April 23).

⚫️ CAKES FROM STIFF OR SOFT HONEY DOUGH

🔸 Properly baked

have a consistent golden or caramel color

▪️ for chocolate cakes, assess doneness based on the baking time of classic cakes, as their dark color makes visual cues challenging

🔸 Underbaked

appear flat, resembling pancakes, indicating insufficient protein and starch denaturation, causing the cakes to collapse

🔸 Overbaked

result in excessive dryness, with the cake edges prone to crumbling

▪️ after soaking, a honey cake with overbaked layers remains moist and tender, while one with underbaked layers tends to be excessively moist and prone to sogginess

⚫️ CAKES FROM LIQUID HONEY DOUGH (VERTICAL LAYER HONEY CAKES)

🔸 Properly baked

display softness, tenderness, and high pliability, making them easy to roll into a roll

🔸 Underbaked

reveal characteristic dark spots, indicating depressions formed due to incomplete protein denaturation

🔸 Overbaked

develop dry areas that crumble, making it challenging to roll such cakes into a roll

Always assess the doneness based on the first cake layer — allow the cake to cool completely, examine its appearance, and adjust the baking time or mode if necessary.

## Tip B2-04: The Physics of Flaky Puff Pastry Formation During Baking

**Body:**

Puff pastry consists of multiple, invisible to the eye, but intact layers of dough, separated from each other by equally thin and intact layers of pliable fat.

As the oven temperature rises, the layers of fat gradually melt. Simultaneously, water within the fat transforms into steam, expanding as the temperature ascends. This steam fills the voids left by the melting fat.

The melted fat acts as a barrier, delaying the escape of steam from the dough. Consequently, under the pressure of the expanding steam, the layers of dough puff up, creating the characteristic flakiness. Meanwhile, the lipids in the fat cause the dough to fry, resulting in a delectably golden-brown and crispy exterior.

Towards the end of the baking process, the dough stabilizes, yielding a final product with numerous delicate layers.

⚠️ The rising of baked goods occurs in the interstices between the layers of dough; however, the individual layers of dough themselves remain flat.

Hence, in areas where the layers of dough make contact, there is minimal flakiness and rising.

Unintentional occurrences of this issue may arise due to:

▪️ uneven rolling of fat between dough layers
▪️ tears in the dough
▪️ cuts in the dough
▪️ premature melting of fat before baking, causing the dough layers to fuse

For easier rolling of fat without damaging dough layers, it is crucial to use pliable fat of similar density to the dough.

If opting for butter, it should be slightly cooled (~16–18 °C) and, optionally, mixed with a small amount of flour.

To prevent tearing, the use of strong flour is recommended, with adequate resting time for the dough between lamination sessions.

Sometimes the dough is intentionally pierced, or small cuts are made in a random fashion to avoid excessive separation. Additionally, pressing along the edges of puff pastry can prevent it from complete separation into individual thin layers during baking.

## Tip B2-06: Blue Spots Between Honey Cake Layers and Filling

**Body:**

On multiple occasions, concerned students have messaged me images of honey cakes exhibiting blue or bluish spots, accompanied by the question: "Marusya, is it safe to eat this? What's going on?"

❓ Can this cake be consumed?
✅ Absolutely

❓ What causes the appearance of blue spots?
❌ Blue spots indicate incomplete chemical reaction of baking soda in the cake layers

To be more specific, these blue spots don't develop within the cake layers themselves but rather at the junctions where the cake and its filling meet.

If baking soda fails to fully react, either during batter preparation or cake baking, the acid in berry fillings and/or sour cream frostings (lactic acid) reacts with the incompletely reacted baking soda in the cakes. Consequently, localized blue spots emerge.

❓ How to eliminate the risk of blue spots

Our primary objective is to ensure that most of the baking soda undergoes the reaction during the batter preparation, and only a small percentage is left for a swift and final reaction in the oven — higher temperatures speed up the process.

But to be on the safe side, after adding baking soda to the heated honey mixture, we can incorporate lemon juice or citric acid to monitor the reaction.

✅ If foaming occurs, albeit weakly, it indicates that the baking soda has already reacted adequately -> remove the mixture from the heat and add flour.

❌ If the mixture foams vigorously, signaling an incomplete reaction between baking soda and the acid in the honey, take corrective measures -> add a small amount of lemon juice or further heat the mixture until the reaction weakens.

❗️Do not allow the mixture to overheat; otherwise, during the addition of flour to the batter, it may partially cook, resulting in a 'rubbery' texture. Similarly, when incorporating eggs into an overheated mixture, there is a risk of protein coagulation. To prevent this, use a water bath and/or actively stir with a whisk while introducing eggs.

## Tip B2-08: The Main Rule for Achieving Perfect Puff Pastry / Functions of Fats in Baking (Part 1)

**Body:**

or

FUNCTIONS OF FATS IN BAKING
part 1

Have you ever thought about why we add butter into cupcake batter? And why do we whip it?

One of the most important functions of fats in baking is to impart softness and tenderness to the baked goods. Today, I'll explain how this works.

Fats serve to coat and envelop the structure formers (gluten proteins, egg whites, and starch granules). They prevent their hydration and the development of structure. Essentially, fats act to shorten the gluten strands, thus preventing the formation of an extensive, interconnected gluten network.

In baking, this quality is often referred to as 'shortness', a term that emphasizes the desired tenderness in the final product.

Fat's impact on baked goods' softness and tenderness depends on:

▪️amount of fat

The higher the fat content, the softer and crumblier the product will be.

All-purpose shortening (confectionery fat) or 100% fat vegetable oils are much more effective at shortening gluten strands compared to, for example, 82% butter, which contains residual water.

▪️fat consistency

The softer and more liquid the fat, the better it softens the batter, resulting in crumblier and more tender baked goods.

Liquid fats, like vegetable oil, blend more readily with the batter, enveloping the flour particles. Soft whipped butter is more effective at softening than unwhipped butter.

▪️size of fat particles

The more thoroughly the fat is incorporated into the flour, the more tender and crumblier the product will be. This occurs as the fat particles become smaller and better cover the structure-forming particles.

▪️presence of emulsifiers

Like thorough dough mixing, emulsifiers assist in even fat distribution throughout baked goods and are highly effective at enveloping the structure-forming particles.

▪️leavening ability of fat or emulsifier

The greater the leavening ability of the fat, the more it contributes to softening. The rising of baked goods during baking stretches and thins the cell walls, weakening them.

Hence, vegetable oil, devoid of leavening properties, softens pie batter but yields denser cakes and tea cakes.

## Tip B2-09: Functions of Fats in Baking (Part 2) — Providing Flakiness, Intro

**Body:**

part 2

Exploring the intricacies of baking is a must for dedicated confectioners who value a thoughtful and precise approach in their culinary endeavors. Understanding the fundamentals of confectionery and the underlying physics of the baking process is essential to avoid unnecessary or incorrect actions in the kitchen.

Let's begin examining the second function of fats in baking.

PROVIDING FLAKINESS

🖊 Flakiness is the ability of baked goods to form numerous thin, flat, and often crispy layers.

🖊 Puff pastry is a culinary product made from numerous thin layers of dough separated by layers of fat, achieved through meticulous folding and rolling. Some puff pastry types have over 80 layers.

VARIETIES OF PUFF PASTRY BY PREPARATION METHOD

🔸 classic
🔸 chopped
🔸 easy-make

In classic puff pastry, fat is repetitively folded and rolled together with the dough (individual puff pastries, croissants, Danish pastries).

In chopped – solid pieces of fat are chopped alongside flour until they resemble crumbs (pies).

In easy-make puff pastry, solid fat, often in a frozen state, is grated coarsely and then swiftly mixed with flour (Napoleon cakes, individual puff pastries).

Irrespective of how fat is incorporated into the dough, whether in pieces or as a single rolled-out layer, the key to forming a quality, flaky puff pastry lies in the subsequent processes of rolling and lamination.

Furthermore, to ensure top-notch puff pastry, the plasticity of the fat is of utmost importance. It is crucial that the consistency of the fat matches that of the dough to prevent it from breaking through the dough layer or being squeezed out.

To prevent the fat from melting entirely during rolling and to maintain its necessary plasticity, it is a common practice to work with the dough at a temperature not exceeding 18 °C.

## Tip B2-10: Functions of Fats in Baking (Part 3) — The Main Rule for Achieving Perfect Puff Pastry

**Body:**

part 3

The main rule for achieving perfect puff pastry is as follows:

☝️the later the fat melts in the oven, the better the flakiness☝️

In chopped pie dough and easy-make puff pastry, the pieces of fat must be well chilled and large in size to ensure that the layers of dough and fat do not mix together.

Hence, when working in a warm pastry shop, the fat should be thoroughly chilled before starting the puff pastry preparation (for easy-make puff pastry, consider freezing it) so that the fat does not completely melt and combine with the flour during the kneading process.

For the same reason, using a mixer is not recommended, as it quickly combines the flour and fat into a homogeneous mass, which diminishes the formation of flakiness during the baking process.

It is also essential to use ice-cold water when kneading puff pastry to prevent the butter in the dough from heating up and melting prematurely.

Similarly, it's imperative to thoroughly chill the dough before rolling it out. The colder the dough and the lower the room temperature, the flakier the final product will be.

Notice how the flaky texture of the dough cannot be matched by its tenderness, a result of the careful combination of fat and flour.

In short, the ability of fat to provide flakiness to baked goods depends on:

▪️ fat hardness: the harder the fat and the higher its melting point, the greater the flakiness

▪️ fat pieces size: larger fat pieces in the dough lead to better flakiness

## Tip B2-12: How and Why to Drain Sour Cream

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

Dear friends, I've received numerous inquiries regarding drained sour cream and where to obtain it🤷‍♀️

Allow me to shed light on this for my beloved followers😘

❓What is drained sour cream

🖊 Drained sour cream is sour cream with artificially increased fat content, achieved by removing some of the moisture, specifically whey, during the draining process.

❓Why drain sour cream

To obtain a harmonious flavor and prolong the shelf life of honey cakes, I recommend using store-bought sour cream.

To get a reliably stable sour cream frosting that is both easy to work with and delightful to the palate, you should aim for sour cream with a fat content of approximately ~36–40%.

However, most store-bought sour creams typically have a maximum fat content of 30%. Consequently, to increase the fat content of sour cream, we must engage in the process of draining.

❓How to drain sour cream

▪️ To obtain drained sour cream, place a strainer over a bowl, ensuring there's adequate space at the base of the bowl to collect any excess liquid.
▪️ Next, place a double layer of cheesecloth within the strainer, pour 30% sour cream into it, and allow the whey to drain for 6–8 hours in the fridge. During this process, 30% sour cream should lose approximately 18% of its whey, thereby increasing its fat content to approximately ~36%.

📎 if the highest fat content available to you is 20–25%, place the sour cream in the fridge to drain for an entire night or a minimum of 10–12 hours. The resulting frosting might not be as stable as with 30% sour cream, but is still suitable for cake assembly.

## Tip B2-13: All About Cake Fillings — Coulis, Confit, Compote (Part 1/2)

**Body:**

I noticed your keen interest in the topic of fillings, so as promised, I'm going to provide you with an in-depth exploration of the 3 most popular fruit fillings: coulis, confit, and compote.

Distinguishing between these fillings might be tricky initially, but I'm here to clarify. Save or write it down for future reference.

🟡 COULIS

The term "coulis" comes from French, meaning "sauce" or "to rub". Now, here's an ⚠️IMPORTANT⚠️ point – coulis can serve a dual role. It can be an independent sauce poured over a finished dessert or function as a filling within a cake.

In the former case, coulis takes the form of a berry or fruit sauce made by blending or "rubbing" the fruits, sometimes thickened with pectin or starch. In the latter case, it is a jelly-like, dense, and stable filling, made from fruit or berry juice, sugar, and a thickening agent. No matter how it's used, coulis consistently imparts a rich flavor derived from the base fruit or berry.

🟡 CONFIT

The French term "confiture" is derived from "confire," meaning "cooking in sugar."

Confiture, or simply confit, is a filling made by simmering fruits or berries in their own juices along with sugar or sugar syrup. Typically, it features whole berries or fruit pieces, although puree can also be used. Its most distinctive characteristic, setting it apart from coulis, is its consistency – confit closely resembles jam, while coulis has a more jelly-like texture.

✒️ the process of boiling fruits or berries in their own juice with sugar or sugar syrup is also known as "candisation"

🟡 COMPOTE

Compote can be either a standalone dessert or a filling inside other desserts.

When enjoyed as a dessert, it consists of berries or fruit pieces simmered in a sugar syrup, sometimes infused with spices. As a filling, it involves cooking berries or fruit pieces with sugar and adding one of thickeners (starch, gelatin, pectin, or agar-agar). The resulting texture is notably dense, akin to jelly or marmalade.

## Tip B2-15: Cake Fillings Part 4 — Curd

**Body:**

Coulis, compote, caramel, confit... What other fillings can we explore for cakes? Naturally, curd comes to mind.

▪️DEFINITION

Curd is a filling that closely resembles custard and can also stand alone as a confectionery creation. It is made by cooking fruit or berry puree or juice with eggs and butter.

▪️MAIN INGREDIENTS

🔸egg yolks
🔸sugar
🔸fruit base
🔸starch
🔸butter

▪️CURD VS CUSTARD

▪️HOW TO MAKE YOUR CURD SPECIAL

🔸to simplify the cooking process, start by dissolving the starch in a few tablespoons of juice beforehand

## Tip B2-20: How to Know When the Custard Is Ready

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

The custard is ready once it has boiled and reached 82°C. Is it so? Yes. Can we rely on this alone? Not at all.

Why?

▪️Firstly, the custard's boiling point can vary between 82 to 85 °C based on the recipe you follow

▪️Secondly, measuring the temperature of boiling custard is challenging for the following reasons:

a) to ensure even cooking, it must be stirred continuously, making it highly inconvenient to simultaneously measure the temperature

b) the presence of air bubbles during boiling can interfere with accurate thermometer readings

▪️Thirdly, it's easy to miss the initial boiling point of custard

When custard is cooked correctly (over very low heat with constant stirring), you may not notice it reaching the boiling point, as bubbles typically form after some time.

⁉️ So, how do you determine when the custard is ready?

I prioritize the thickening process. The custard should reach a complete and uniform thickness with no liquid or thicker parts remaining – it should thicken evenly throughout.

After achieving this complete thickness, I continue to stir constantly and let it boil for an additional one to two minutes, adjusting the duration based on the quantity of custard, to ensure there are no uncooked areas remaining.

## Tip B2-22: When the Cheesecake Is Ready: Egg White Coagulation (Part 1)

**Body:**

#marusya_about_coagulation

Through numerous experiments and master classes, I stumbled upon a fascinating observation – cheesecakes with different fillings require varying baking times.

Cheesecake can be seen as an adaptation of the basic custard recipe, incorporating eggs as both a thickener and gelling agent.

As the eggs are subjected to heat, the protein structure starts to denature and unfold. These unfolded protein molecules then interact and connect with one another, creating a strong yet highly flexible network.

This network effectively binds water and other liquids within the cheesecake mixture, ultimately signaling that the product is properly cooked.

## Tip B2-23: Egg White Coagulation Part 2 — Quantity of Eggs, Cooking Temperature, Part of Egg Used

**Body:**

Today we continue the article on egg white coagulation.

Discover the beginning at #marusya_about_coagulation

The rate of protein coagulation, and consequently, the cooking time of cheesecake, creme brulee, or custard, depends on:

▪️quantity or ratio of eggs

Normally, egg proteins coagulate at 70 °C; however, if the eggs are mixed with milk, water, or other ingredients, the coagulation temperature rises.

As an illustration, the proteins in custard coagulate at 82–85 °C. The addition of milk, cream, or sugar makes it more challenging for them to collide and form bonds.

So, the greater the amount of eggs in the mixture, the quicker it will cook.

▪️cooking temperatures

Egg coagulation is not an instantaneous process; it requires some time to occur. The higher the temperature, the faster the mixture cooks, and therefore, the proteins fully coagulate in less time.

Nevertheless, when eggs coagulate too quickly, the proteins may not have sufficient time to unfold, leading to an issue where the mixture might not fully thicken.

For instance, if custard is cooked over high heat, not only does it risk curdling and burning, but there's also a likelihood that it won't reach the desired thickness.

For enhanced thickening, cook it on low heat while continuously stirring. This is why water baths are so good for making custard, pudding, and cheesecakes.

Water baths maintain temperatures below the boiling point (at 82–88 °C), even when the oven temperature is set at 165 °C or higher. With a water bath, the coagulation process slows down, and the baking process becomes more uniform, preventing the outer layer of the cheesecake from becoming overly tight, rubbery, or curdled while ensuring proper baking on the inside.

▪️part of the egg used

Egg yolks coagulate at higher temperatures (65–70 °C) compared to egg whites (60–65 °C), resulting in a lower risk of protein hypercoagulation (folding) when using only yolks.

It's because the proteins in the yolks are lipoproteins bound to fats and emulsifiers, which complicates the protein binding process.

## Tip B2-24: Egg White Coagulation Part 3 — Amount of Sugar, Amount of Lipids, Presence of Acid

**Body:**

#marusya_about_coagulation

In the previous parts, we've already discussed that the rate of protein coagulation depends on the quantity or ratio of eggs, the cooking temperatures, and the part of the egg used.

However, these are not the only factors.

The rate of coagulation, and consequently the cooking time of the cheesecake, will also be influenced by:

▪️ amount of sugar

Apart from its role in diluting protein molecules, sugar also slows down the coagulation of the egg whites, preventing the proteins from unfolding.

This means that sugar aids in preventing the curdling of egg whites. Hence, the egg filling in quiche, which lacks added sugar, tends to curdle after baking and release liquid.

Consequently, it's not surprising that sugar is considered a tenderizing agent in baking. By slowing down coagulation, sugar also retards the formation of the egg structure, as well as the networks of gluten and starch.

When a sufficient amount of sugar is present, it can stop coagulation entirely, potentially leading to the product appearing undercooked despite prolonged cooking.

▪️amounts of lipids (fats)

Similar to sugar, lipids (fats, oils, and emulsifiers) slow down the coagulation process of proteins through direct interaction with them.

This explains why custards made with cream and yolks possess a silkier, softer, and creamier texture compared to those prepared with milk and whole eggs.

▪️presence and amount of acid

Acids accelerate coagulation by reducing the temperature required for this process.

📎 Acids in cheesecake or custard can stem from sources such as lemon juice, various fruits, raisins, or fermented dairy products.

This explains why fruit and berry cheesecakes bake more quickly than their counterparts.

## Tip B2-25: Egg White Coagulation Part 4 — Starch, Salt, Proteolytic Enzymes

**Body:**

part 4, the last one

#marusya_about_coagulation

So, here are the last three 🤫secret factors🤫 that affect the cooking time of cheesecake:

▪️presence and quantity of starch

Starch increases the temperature of protein coagulation, interfering with the process.

As an example, let's compare the process of making custard sauce (without starch) and custard (with starch or flour).

The custard must be brought to boiling temperature (~85 °C) and boiled for 2 minutes or more. The custard sauce will curdle before it reaches boiling point.

Always make sure that custard containing starch is cooked properly. If it is not cooked long enough, not only will the starch not jellify (gelatinize) properly, but the small amount of amylase that is present in egg yolks may not be inactivated.

🖊Amylase is an enzyme that hydrolyzes starch to produce simple sugars. Unless completely deactivated, amylase will easily liquefy the custard overnight.

▪️amount of salt

Salt in hard water and dairy products, or adding a small amount of table salt, speeds up and enhances the coagulation of proteins in the egg.

Milk proteins can also interact with egg proteins to enhance gel formation. If you make the custard with water instead of milk or cream, it will turn out very liquid, barely thickened.

▪️presence of proteolytic enzymes

Enzymes break down egg proteins in the same way they break down the proteins in gelatin.

If you add raw pineapple with active protease to custard or cheesecake, it will not thicken. If you first boil the pineapple, thereby inactivating the protease, the intact egg proteins in the custard will coagulate and it will thicken.

## Tip B2-27: Cultured Butter in Napoleon Cake Layers

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

The two most common butter types are sweet cream and cultured butter. So, what is the difference between them?

🖊Sweet cream butter is made from pasteurized cream. It has a gentle milky flavor and aroma.

🖊Cultured butter, on the other hand, is made from pasteurized fermented cream. It involves the use of pure cultures of lactic acid bacteria during fermentation. This variety of butter has a tangier, more complex flavor and aroma.

In cases where the packaging doesn't specify the type of butter you have, you can distinguish between them by examining their composition. Cultured butter can be readily identified by the presence of lactic acid bacteria, whereas sweet cream butter consists solely of cream.

And that is the

❗️MELTING POINT❗️

Sweet cream butter – 32-33 °C
Cultured butter – 34-35 °C

When making layers for the Napoleon cake, our aim is to maintain the butter's low temperature for as long as possible, preventing it from melting due to ambient conditions and the warmth of our hands while grating it during dough kneading. This approach is crucial for achieving the ultra-thin and flaky puff pastry.

Here, a mere difference of a few degrees plays a significant role, particularly when working in a warm cooking environment.

📎 before grating cultured butter, I place it in the freezer overnight, and while grating, I consistently coat it with flour

📎 while it's possible to make puff pastry using sweet cream butter, the resulting pastry will be less flaky

## Tip B2-31: Why Should Cream Be Cooled Before Whipping?

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

Whipped cream is an essential ingredient in most desserts. Cream with a fat content of at least 33% is considered ideal for whipping. Have you ever wondered why?🤔

The fats in the cream crystallize around the air bubbles that form during whipping. If the cream is low-fat, there will be too few crystals to hold air bubbles, and the cream won't whip.

However, even when whipping heavy cream, instead of a stable lush foam and beautiful stable peaks, there is a risk of getting either an unstable fluid or split mass.

The fact is that one of the most important factors affecting the quality of whipping cream is temperature: the cream must be cooled before whipping. In short, the crystallization of fats occurs only in a cold environment – frosting made of insufficiently cold cream will deflate.

To understand why this happens, you need to delve into the physics and chemistry of the process.

📎 Cream is stabilized by milk fat, which is presented in the form of fat grains – the so-called globules, surrounded by a protective membrane. In the process of whipping cold cream, the whisk begins to destroy the integrity of the membranes that surround the globule, and partially exposes the fat component. Globules with their bare parts begin to be attracted either to each other or to air, thus forming a fat network inside which air bubbles are sealed.

🖇This property of the globules allows the cream to expand and turn into a light, airy mass. At higher temperatures, fat grains melt, become liquid, and repel air, so warm cream cannot whip properly, and it takes longer to reach maximum volume.

❌ But, the longer the cream is whipped, the more the protective membrane is destroyed. As a result, bare fat particles begin to strive only towards themselves – they displace air, clump into a dense mass, repel water, which results in the cream with a grainy texture.

## Tip B2-34: How Moisture Comes in Contact With Chocolate

**Body:**

part 1

#marusya_chocolate_storage

There are a lot of controversies and rumors regarding the best way to store chocolate. This means that this topic is interesting and important. Let's sort things out!

Basically, the key to long-term chocolate storage comes down to just two things: temperature and humidity. Chocolate is best stored at cool ambient temperatures (15-21°C) to prevent fat bloom or melting, and in a dry place (70% humidity or below) to keep it from interacting with water and developing sugar bloom or seizing.

But let's dig deeper and figure out all the subtleties and nuances, as we're used to make things clear, and hope you've already managed to fall in love with that too🤎

So, here's the first question that we will consider:

🟤 HOW LONG DOES CHOCOLATE LAST?

Usually, chocolate has an expiration date. But it only serves as a general guideline set by manufacturers as a best before date, until which they can guarantee the best taste and texture of chocolate.

In fact, chocolate can be stored much longer than the use-by date indicated on the package:

🍫 Dark chocolate – can maintain its quality 2 years from the manufacturing date in proper ambient storage conditions, and up to 4 years refrigerated with some degradation.
🍫 White chocolate – can maintain its quality up to 1 year from its manufacturing date in proper ambient storage conditions, and up to 2 years refrigerated with some degradation.
🍫 Milk chocolate – the same as with white chocolate.

🟤 Firstly, chocolate has the ability to absorb moisture!

Dark chocolate starts absorbing moisture when the humidity level exceeds 82%, whereas milk chocolate is susceptible to moisture absorption above 78%. Thus, to preserve the delightful appearance of chocolate, it is advisable to maintain an environmental humidity level of 70% or below.

🟤 Secondly, condensation may develop on the chocolate.

This can occur due to two factors:

1. Low storage temperature, such as when chocolate is stored in the fridge.

2. Sudden temperature fluctuations exceeding 10°C, such as when chocolate is transitioned from a cold place to a warmer one.

The reasons for moisture coming in contact with chocolate can differ, but the outcome remains consistent: the sugar within the chocolate dissolves in the water and migrates to the surface. As the moisture evaporates, tiny white crystals become visible, ultimately forming the distinctive "dusty" surface.

So let's summarize. In order to prevent sugar bloom in chocolate, we should:

▪️ store chocolate in a place with a consistently stable temperature
▪️ avoid rapid temperature fluctuations of more than 10 °C within a short span of time
▪️ prevent any liquid from coming into contact with the chocolate

## Tip B2-41: Why One Shouldn't Open the Oven While Baking a Sponge

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

If you open the oven while baking the sponge, the sponge will deflate, it's true. But why does this happen? Have you ever thought about it?

Let's figure it out.

Factors to consider:

▪️ the presence of baking powder in the recipe
▪️ the presence of water in the recipe (in the composition of eggs)
▪️ the presence of protein and starch (in the recipe or in the composition of the baking powder)
▪️ incorporated air
▪️ heating to high temperatures

Now let's have a closer look at each of the factors, step by step. Follow me.

🌬 By whipping the egg whites with sugar, we incorporate air into the meringue (and subsequently, into our sponge batter).

💧 When the baking powder is added along with the rest of the dry ingredients to the meringue, it reacts with the water contained in the eggs, and a neutralization reaction occurs, during which the carbon dioxide is released.

🔥Then, when we bake the sponge, this is what happens in the oven:

🗿 Under the influence of high temperatures, the process of protein and starch denaturation occurs, and they change from a liquid into a solid state

🫧 air bubbles, or bubbles of carbon dioxide, like any other gas, expand when heated (you might remember this from Physics lessons at school) and remain in the form of cavities in the sponge, in other words, make it porous, and after cooling, carbon dioxide and steam come out from these cavities, and they are filled with air

❌ If you open the oven while baking the sponge, the temperature in it will drop sharply, the gas will shrink back, the bubbles will become smaller, and if the protein has not yet had time to denature, the sponge will deflate.

## Tip B2-42: Lumpy Cream Cheese Mixture: Why Does It Happen and How to Prevent It?

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

Once, at a masterclass, I approached a student and, seeing her cheesecake mixture, asked: "Have you used a sieve?", she replied: "Yes, how do you know?"

Guys, it's very simple — even if you push a lumpy cream cheese mixture through a sieve, you still will have a lumpy mixture. The only difference will be the size of the lumps which will be equal to the diameter of the sieve mesh.

Therefore, there are 2 answers to the question of why the cheesecake mixture is lumpy:

✖️ the mixture wasn't properly mixed
✖️ the mixture wasn't properly mixed + you tried to fix it in the wrong way

To prevent lumps in the cheesecake mixture, one should adhere to the following rules ⬇️

☑️ Use a flex edge flat beater

Don't mix the cheesecake mixture with a whisk attachment — firstly, the cream cheese will get caught up in the whisk's wires and lumps will form; secondly, the whisk will incorporate lots of air into the mixture, and as a result, you won't be able to fit all the mixture into the crust, so after baking the cheesecake will sink in the center.

📎 You can also use a paddle attachment, however, you'll have to periodically stop the mixer and scrape the cream cheese from the sides of the bowl and the attachment itself, while the flex edge flat beater removes the mixture from the sides of the bowl during mixing

☑️ Stick to the sequence of adding the ingredients:

🔽 cream cheese
🔽 sugar
🔽 eggs
🔽 flavoring additives (purees, ganaches, pastes)
🔽 cream, milk, alcohol

First, mix the cream cheese and sugar (if listed in the recipe) into a pasty consistency. Next, pour in the egg melange in a thin stream, or add the egg yolks one at a time, mixing thoroughly after each addition.

🖊 Egg melange is a homogeneous mass of eggs combined with a whisk or blender

Then add the flavorings, and lastly — the liquid ingredients.

📎 if the flavorings have a very thick consistency, e.g. dulce de leche, thick nut paste, it's better to add them before you start introducing the eggs
