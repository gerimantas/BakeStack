## Tip B3-01 — Flour Glossary: Buckwheat / Oat / Teff Flour (tail end of "60 Shades of Flour" series)

**Source lines:** 2133–2145

**Body:**

Contains 4 times more fiber than the all-purpose flour —> absorbs more moisture —> requires more liquid ingredients —> more moist and dense baked goods.

BUCKWHEAT FLOUR

Made by grinding buckwheat seeds. Buckwheat is a pseudocereal that has nothing to do with wheat; it's actually a fruit seed related to sorrel and rhubarb. Buckwheat flour is gluten-free. In recipes, it's most often combined with regular flour and other types of gluten-free flours. Best used in yeast-free pastries.

OAT FLOUR

A gluten-free flour made by grinding whole oat groats (hulled grains) into a fine powder. It has a slightly denser texture than regular flour. Contains 3 times more fiber than the all-purpose flour. Oat flour makes delicious banana bread, muffins, and cookies.

TEFF FLOUR

One of the most unusual types of flour, obtained from ancient grain native to Ethiopia, the Eragrostis teff. Because the grains of this plant are very small, teff flour contains more bran and germ than other whole grains, making it a good source of amino acids, iron, fiber, and calcium. It has a unique taste with pleasant nutty notes. The aroma of waffles or crepes made with teff flour will make any breakfast unforgettable!

## Tip B3-05 — Life Hack: How to Know When Zephyr Syrup Is Cooked Without a Thermometer

**Source lines:** 2250–2270

**Body:**

LIFE HACK: HOW TO KNOW WHEN THE SYRUP FOR ZEPHYR IS COOKED WITHOUT A THERMOMETER

#marusya_theory_tips

Experienced zephyr makers know how important it is to start adding boiling syrup to the whipped berry puree in time — if the syrup is undercooked, the zephyr mass will not be thick enough, and if it's overcooked, the zephyr will quickly crystallize, and when tasting, the sugar will crunch on your teeth.

How to determine that very moment when the syrup is cooked?

There's a magic number — 110 °C. It is up to this temperature that it is recommended to cook syrup for zephyr. But I'd say that this number is literally "magic" — it's very difficult to correctly measure the temperature of a boiling syrup:

▫️ firstly, the syrup actively boils, which makes it impossible to correctly measure the temperature on the surface of the syrup using a pyrometer

▫️ secondly, there should be a certain minimum amount of the liquid in the saucepan so that the probe thermometer can correctly read the temperature, and if you don't make zephyrs in large batches, a small amount of syrup, which is usually required to make them, won't allow you to do that

Therefore, I never determine the doneness of the syrup by temperature — only by consistency! But how to do that?

💡 Here's a life hack for you!

Take a small amount of syrup with a spatula and raise it to eye level: if the syrup flows down in a thin stream, then the syrup is ready.

📎 In the carousel, you can find a short video from the live broadcast on the preparation of zephyr, where I clearly show how to check the doneness of syrup visually.

## Tip B3-06 — Why One Shouldn't Open the Oven While Baking a Sponge

**Source lines:** 2271–2299

**Body:**

WHY ONE SHOULDN'T OPEN THE OVEN WHILE BAKING A SPONGE

#marusya_theory_tips

If you open the oven while baking the sponge, the sponge will deflate, it's true. But why does this happen? Have you ever thought about it?

Let's figure it out.

Factors to consider:

▪️ the presence of baking powder in the recipe
▪️ the presence of water in the recipe (in the composition of eggs)
▪️ the presence of protein and starch (in the recipe or in the composition of the baking powder)
▪️ incorporated air
▪️ heating to high temperatures

Now let's have a closer look at each of the factors, step by step. Follow me.

🌬 By whipping the egg whites with sugar, we incorporate air into the meringue (and subsequently, into our sponge batter).

💧 When the baking powder is added along with the rest of the dry ingredients to the meringue, it reacts with the water contained in the eggs, and a neutralization reaction occurs, during which the carbon dioxide is released.

🔥Then, when we bake the sponge, this is what happens in the oven:

🗿 Under the influence of high temperatures, the process of protein and starch denaturation occurs, and they change from a liquid into a solid state

🫧 air bubbles, or bubbles of carbon dioxide, like any other gas, expand when heated (you might remember this from Physics lessons at school) and remain in the form of cavities in the sponge, in other words, make it porous, and after cooling, carbon dioxide and steam come out from these cavities, and they are filled with air

❌ If you open the oven while baking the sponge, the temperature in it will drop sharply, the gas will shrink back, the bubbles will become smaller, and if the protein has not yet had time to denature, the sponge will deflate.

## Tip B3-07 — Lumpy Cream Cheese Mixture: Why Does It Happen and How to Prevent It?

**Source lines:** 2300–2335

**Body:**

LUMPY CREAM CHEESE MIXTURE: WHY DOES IT HAPPEN AND HOW TO PREVENT IT?

#marusya_theory_tips

Once, at a masterclass, I approached a student and, seeing her cheesecake mixture, asked: "Have you used a sieve?", she replied: "Yes, how do you know?"

Guys, it's very simple — even if you push a lumpy cream cheese mixture through a sieve, you still will have a lumpy mixture. The only difference will be the size of the lumps which will be equal to the diameter of the sieve mesh.

Therefore, there are 2 answers to the question of why the cheesecake mixture is lumpy:

✖️ the mixture wasn't properly mixed
✖️ the mixture wasn't properly mixed + you tried to fix it in the wrong way

To prevent lumps in the cheesecake mixture, one should adhere to the following rules ⬇️

☑️ Use a flex edge flat beater

Don't mix the cheesecake mixture with a whisk attachment — firstly, the cream cheese will get caught up in the whisk's wires and lumps will form; secondly, the whisk will incorporate lots of air into the mixture, and as a result, you won't be able to fit all the mixture into the crust, so after baking the cheesecake will sink in the center.

📎 You can also use a paddle attachment, however, you'll have to periodically stop the mixer and scrape the cream cheese from the sides of the bowl and the attachment itself, while the flex edge flat beater removes the mixture from the sides of the bowl during mixing

☑️ Stick to the sequence of adding the ingredients:

🔽 cream cheese
🔽 sugar
🔽 eggs
🔽 flavoring additives (purees, ganaches, pastes)
🔽 cream, milk, alcohol

First, mix the cream cheese and sugar (if listed in the recipe) into a pasty consistency. Next, pour in the egg melange in a thin stream, or add the egg yolks one at a time, mixing thoroughly after each addition.

🖊 Egg melange is a homogeneous mass of eggs combined with a whisk or blender

Then add the flavorings, and lastly — the liquid ingredients.

📎 if the flavorings have a very thick consistency, e.g. dulce de leche, thick nut paste, it's better to add them before you start introducing the eggs

## Tip B3-08 — Honey Dough: 3 Consistencies You Should Know About

**Source lines:** 2336–2378

**Body:**

HONEY DOUGH: 3 CONSISTENCIES YOU SHOULD KNOW ABOUT

If you still think that a honey cake is an uninteresting dessert that can only amaze you with the number of cake layers, just scroll through my feed and the myth will be dispelled instantly.

How to get such appetizing cross sections?

One of the keys is the optimal consistency of honey dough for each particular honey cake. Let me cover the crucial questions.

What determines the consistency of honey dough?
✔️ the amount of flour in its composition — the less flour you use, the more liquid and / or sticky the dough will be

❓What are the types of honey dough consistencies?
✔️ 1. Stiff
✔️ 2. Soft
✔️ 3. Liquid

STIFF HONEY DOUGH:

▪️obtained by adding a moderate amount of flour
▪️should be slightly sticky when mixed
▪️ready to work with (!!!) only after sitting for 15–20 minutes at room temperature — the fats contained in butter stabilize and the dough becomes non-sticky
▪️rolled out with a rolling pin
▪️easy, almost effortless to roll out

SOFT HONEY DOUGH:

▪️used for cake layers with a reduced flour content
▪️turns out very sticky when mixed
▪️ready to work with after 4–12 hours in the fridge (depending on the recipe) — during this time the fats become solid and stabilize completely –> the dough becomes non-sticky
▪️easy to roll out with a rolling pin

LIQUID HONEY DOUGH (BATTER):

▪️contains the least amount of flour
▪️ready to work with immediately after mixing
▪️cannot be rolled out — for this batter you'll need an offset spatula — evenly spread the batter over the parchment paper, giving it the desired shape

❓What does the consistency of honey dough affect?
✔️ the texture of finished cake layers

▪️ crispy and crumbly cake layers are obtained from stiff dough
▪️ soft dough ensures tender, soft and slightly airy cake layers
▪️ honey batter helps to get elastic and at the same time very pliable cake layers

## Tip B3-10 — What You Need to Know About Mousses, Part 1: Mousse Base & Aerator (Meringue)

**Source lines:** 2414–2460

**Body:**

WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW ABOUT MOUSSES
part 1

#marusya_about_mousse

Guys, today I'm presenting to you a new series of posts. Let's learn the science of making mousses from A to Z so you can feel confident when making them.

Let's start, as always, with the basics. What is a mousse?

Mousse (from French 'mousse' – "foam") is a dish or a filling for cakes and other desserts with the consistency of an airy, light foam, composed of:

1️⃣ flavoring component (base)
2️⃣ whipped component (aerator)
3️⃣ thickener

Now let's take a closer look at each component.

✅ MOUSSE BASE is the main flavoring component which can be:

🔸 custard
🔸 ganache
🔸 fruit puree
🔸 creamy caramel
🔸 nut or flower paste
🔸 yogurt or fermented baked milk (ryazhenka)
🔸 coffee/tea/alcohol

There are mousses with a "pure" base, i.e. only one ingredient is used, for example, caramel. But in my opinion, the most interesting are mousses with complex bases – you can combine coffee with fruit puree, caramel with nut paste or even crème anglaise with alcohol!

The mousse base takes up ~35-55% of the total mass, depending on the thickness of the base and the desired consistency of the finished mousse.

✅ WHIPPED COMPONENT OR AERATOR is responsible for airiness and lightness of the mousse. These aerators can be:

🔸 whipping cream
🔸 meringue
🔸 pâte à bombe

🖊 Pâte à bombe – a cream made of whipped egg yolks and boiled sugar syrup.

Before being added to the mousse, any of these ingredients is whipped to incorporate air bubbles, which helps to make the product airy and fluffy.

Meringue-based mousses

🔺most often, the Italian meringue is used, as it's the most stable of all types of meringue
🔺contain the greatest number of air bubbles, and therefore are the most unstable, soft and delicate
🔺 have the shortest shelf life
🔺 keep maximum taste of the flavoring base

## Tip B3-11 — What You Need to Know About Mousses, Part 2: Cream-Based Mousses & Thickener (Gelatin)

**Source lines:** 2461–2494

**Body:**

part 2

#marusya_about_mousse

Thanks to a large number of pluses under yesterday's post, I decided to immediately write a continuation – it's always nice to see mutual interest❤️

Cream-based mousses

🔺 make the taste of the mousse base less prominent, unlike meringue-based mousses
🔺 more stable and thicker than those made with meringue
🔺 use cold 30–33% cream
🔺 whip it into soft peaks at medium speed – fast whipping leads to a less stable foam
🔺 stick to the whipping temperature – 0–5 °C – if the temperature is at least 3–4 °C higher, the cream will whip much worse or may even curdle
🔺 it's important not to over-whip the cream, otherwise it'll be difficult to combine it with the base, and the texture of the mousse won't be perfect
🔺 the temperature of the base to which we add the whipped cream shouldn't exceed 35 °C, otherwise the mousse will turn out to be too liquid

It's not common to use pure pâte à bombe to make mousses – most often it's combined with cream or meringue.

In general, in one mousse you can use:

▪️whipped cream and meringue
▪️meringue and pâte à bombe
▪️whipped cream and pâte à bombe
▪️whipped cream, meringue and pâte à bombe

The whipped component can take up around 35–60% of the total mass of the mousse, depending on the thickness of the base, the type of aeration and the desired consistency of the finished mousse.

✅ THICKENER: traditionally, mousses are made with gelatin. However, some of the recipes call for agar-agar or cocoa butter, or gelatin + cocoa butter. The more gelatin you add, the thicker the mousse will be, and vice versa.

Gelatin can take up 0.5–2% of the total mass of the mousse:

▪️ if the mousse contains alcohol, tart fruits or lots of air (meringue-based mousses), the percentage of gelatin will be around 2%
▪️ mousses made from milk or white couvertures (chocolate with a high content of cocoa butter), contain around 0.5% gelatin
▪️if it's a chocolate-based mousse which contains enough cocoa butter (couverture/dark/bitter chocolate), you can skip adding the gelatin

## Tip B3-14 — How to Temper Eggs and Why You Need To, Part 2: Why & How Much Liquid

**Source lines:** 2551–2574

**Body:**

part 2

#marusya_theory_tips

In a previous post, we discussed with you what tempering eggs is. So where does this process take place?

In fact, it's quite simple and, most likely, you have already tempered eggs without knowing it. We temper eggs when we prepare the base for custards, pudding, ice cream, flan, crème brûlée, etc.

In the preparation of any custard, for example, there's a step of trickling hot milk or cream into the egg mixture — when gradually adding a hot liquid to the eggs, we thereby temper them.

🔴 WHY WE NEED TO TEMPER EGGS

Tempering eggs prevents the proteins from coagulating. Eggs are very sensitive to temperature. If you combine the eggs with hot liquid in one go, they will curdle. Therefore, when tempering, 2 rules must be observed:

✔️ add the hot liquid gradually, in a thin stream
✔️ constantly stir the mixture with a whisk

Lastly, let's analyze one more interesting nuance.

🔴 HOW MUCH HOT LIQUID SHOULD BE ADDED TO EGGS

Tempering is a means of getting something (eggs) from point A to point B (from cold to hot) slowly and steadily. And is a technique, not a scientific formula, which is why there's no definitive answer for this question.

## Tip B3-20 — A Crash Course in Chocolate, Part 3: Tempering Definition & Classic Method

**Source lines:** 2779–2824

**Body:**

part 3

In the previous posts — #marusya_about_chocolate — we have covered the basics: definition of chocolate, its types, composition and brands.

With this knowledge, we can proceed to the next step — tempering.

WHAT IS TEMPERING?

Tempering, or crystallization, is the process of heating and cooling chocolate to certain temperatures in order to obtain stable cocoa butter crystals, which gives the chocolate a number of important properties:

✔️gives chocolate a glossy finish and make it snap cleanly
✔️allows you to avoid undesirable imperfections on the surface of chocolate:
▪️ sugar bloom — a whitish film what affects the tasting
▪️ fat bloom — greyish or whitish streaks or spots of cocoa butter
✔️chocolate hardens much faster
✔️ stays solid in warmer temperatures, as it has a higher melting point.

METHODS FOR TEMPERING CHOCOLATE:

✔️classic (tabling)
✔️seeding (callets)
✔️Mycryo — powdered cocoa butter

CLASSIC TEMPERING METHOD

The method requires you to use a marble or granite slab/countertop. Since not everyone has an access to it, I won't dwell on it in detail. But the technique itself is important for understanding the processes:

Destroy the solid form of chocolate by bringing it to the melting point:
▪️dark — 50-55°C
▪️milk — 45-50°C
▪️white — 40-45°C

✔️Bring it down to the crystallization temperature of cocoa butter — 27°C

✔️Bring the chocolate to its working temperature:
▪️dark — 31-32°C
▪️milk — 30-31°C
▪️white — 29-30°C

✔️Give liquid chocolate the desired shape.

✔️Let the chocolate stabilize and become solid again.

SEEDING METHOD OR CALLET TEMPERING

The trick is that the callets themselves are already tempered. Cocoa butter crystals are already crystallized, that is, step 2 from the classic method is omitted. All you need to do is melt the chocolate and bring it to working temperature.

## Tip B3-21 — A Crash Course in Chocolate, Part 4: Seeding Method Detail

**Source lines:** 2825–2857

**Body:**

part 4

We continue a detailed analysis of the methods for tempering chocolate.

As you remember from the previous post — #marusya_about_chocolate — the classic method is not the easiest one, since it requires lots of effort as well as marble or granite slab/countertop. But at the same time, this is the only way to temper chocolate in large quantities (over 1 kg).

In large scale production where it's often necessary to temper 1-2 kg of chocolate or even more, they use special tempering machines that maintain the required temperature of chocolate.

For home bakers, one of the best methods is the seeding method or callet tempering. Let's learn how it works.

SEEDING METHOD OR CALLET TEMPERING

Destroy the solid form of chocolate by bringing it to the melting point:
▪️dark — 50–55 °C
▪️milk — 45–50 °C
▪️white — 40–45 °C

✔️Add chocolate callets and stir to bring the chocolate to its working temperature:
▪️dark — 31–32 °C
▪️milk — 30–31 °C
▪️white — 29–30 °C

And voilà — you can give liquid chocolate the desired shape👌

With this method there is no need to first bring chocolate down to the crystallization temperature of cocoa butter (27 °C), and then bring it up to the working temperature, as with the classic method, since the callets themselves are already tempered and have a stable crystalline structure.

❓If I use chocolate callets only, then I just melt them and that's it?

❌ No!

Even if you use only chocolate in callets, bringing it to working temperature is not enough — heat treatment destroys the bonds between cocoa butter molecules, the crystalline structure breaks up and collapses.

That's why it is necessary to add cold callets with a stable crystalline structure to melted chocolate, as they prompt the chain reaction resulting in properly crystallized chocolate.

## Tip B3-22 — A Crash Course in Chocolate, Part 5: Secrets of Seeding Method (Melting Tips)

**Source lines:** 2858–2879

**Body:**

part 5

Secrets of tempering chocolate using seeding method (callets)

1️⃣ It is best to melt chocolate in the microwave. Chocolate is very hygroscopic, meaning it absorbs moisture from the air. Therefore, a water bath will not be the best option for melting chocolate.

2️⃣ To avoid overheating, melt the chocolate in the microwave in 20 to 30 second intervals, thoroughly stirring it with a spatula between each interval.

📍 melt the white chocolate on the lowest power level and in very short, 10-20 second intervals as it can be easily overheated

3️⃣ I do not recommend melting chocolate in glassware — glass retains temperature for a long time and gives it to chocolate even after we've already brought it to 50-55 °C (45-50 °C milk and 40-45 °C white chocolate). Opt for plastic vessels instead.

4️⃣ Chocolate must be constantly stirred! This is necessary in order to ensure the same temperature of chocolate at the bottom, sides and in the middle of the bowl, as well as to correctly measure the temperature. It is most convenient to measure the temperature of chocolate with a pyrometer.

5️⃣ In order to bring the chocolate to its working temperature (dark — 31-32 °C, milk — 30-31 °C, white — 29-30 °C), add callets to the melted chocolate (20-25% of the total volume). With this amount, stable crystals will prompt the chain reaction resulting in properly crystallized chocolate.

This ratio is considered optimal, but since there are many variables in this equation, I would call it only approximate. You can be guided by it, but make sure to always keep an eye on the temperature of the chocolate.

6️⃣ When you get down to work, please remember that:

🔺 You need to work with chocolate quickly — if the chocolate is properly tempered, it will set in 3 minutes at a temperature of about 20 °C
🔺 white chocolate is much thicker than dark or milk chocolate — to make it easier to work with, add a little cocoa butter along with the callets to it

🟤 WHAT IS MYCRYO?

Mycryo® is the brand name of the Barry Callebaut group of companies, which specializes in the production of a unique product — pure cocoa butter in powder form.

This 100% vegetable fat is an absolutely healthy product, mostly thanks to cocoa beans: it contains no cholesterol, has low calorie content, and you need less fat for frying.

🔺brings out the natural flavors of even the most delicate ingredients, and unlike other fats, it does not dominate over them
🔺 and, of course, facilitates the process of chocolate tempering

🟤 HOW IS MYCRYO OBTAINED?

Cocoa butter, obtained through roasting and grinding cocoa beans, is then frozen at very low temperatures to produce a dry powder. In other words, atomization occurs — dry powder is cooled to approximately -250 °C.

As a result, cocoa butter takes the form of granules, which are pure beta crystals, which are essential for proper chocolate tempering.

Let's try to figure it out.

🟤 TYPES OF COCOA BUTTER CRYSTALS

So, there are six types of cocoa butter crystals, each with a name: γ‎ (gamma), α (alpha), β'' (beta 2), β' (beta prime), β (beta) and super-β (super-beta).

They differ from each other by the strength of the bonds between the molecules. The stronger the bonds are, the more energy (higher temperature) is needed to break these bonds.

Of all six crystals, only one fits for tempering — beta (β) — they are the most stable and form the correct crystal lattice.

## Tip B3-24 — All You Need to Know About Starch

ALL YOU NEED TO KNOW ABOUT STARCH

#marusya_about _starch

We've already talked about pectin, agar-agar and gelatin. So today I suggest talking about the most popular thickening agent — starch.

In fact, there are lots of types of starch: potato, rice, wheat, corn, tapioca, sorghum, etc. However, the most common types are potato and cornstarch.

It is preferable to use cornstarch for making desserts, and it's not recommended to replace it with potato starch. Cornstarch ensures a more delicate texture, while adding potato starch results in a much thicker texture.

Potato starch
🔸 ensures a more fluid texture
🔸 has a characteristic flavor
🔸 the starch mixture is transparent
🔸 may impart a bluish color to some dishes

Cornstarch
🔸 ensures a more viscous texture
🔸 has no aftertaste
🔸 the starch mixture is turbid
🔸 does not change the color of the dish

Types of cornstarch
✔️regular cornstarch — does not contain impurities and has a white color
✔️modified starch — a safe product, additionally processed for better absorption

Take into account that❗️starch as a thickener activates properly only after bringing the mixture to a boil❗️

The boiling point of mixtures with starch added is 82 °C.

Bring the filling or custard with cornstarch to a boil, then, stirring vigorously with a spatula, allow it to cook over low heat for several minutes so that the starch has completely denatured — subjected to a heat treatment, the starch granules should swell and change their structure.

If the starch has not completely swollen, after cooling, the filling won't be stable and may leak water, acquiring a liquid, runny structure.

The dosage of starch may be different depending on:

✔️ density of the product to be thickened: liquid with a high percentage of water or a thicker puree with a high content of natural pectin
✔️ consistency of the filling you want to get

## Tip B3-27 — Honey: Myths vs. Reality, Part 1: Is Heated Honey Toxic? (HMF Intro)

**Source lines:** 3056–3090

**Body:**

HONEY. MYTHS VS REALITY

part 1

Since without honey a honey cake is no longer a honey cake, I've been working with this product a lot for at least several years.

Therefore, I've faced a wide range of questions regarding this ingredient. The most popular of them are the following:

🌿 Can honey be heated? Doesn't it become toxic after this?

🌿 What are the substitutes for honey in recipes and how to use them correctly?

Honey is a healthy and natural product. It contains glucose, fructose, a small amount of sucrose, flavonoids, microelements and enzymes. It has bactericidal and medicinal properties.

📌 So, the first question and the most common myth at the same time.

❗️ When heated, honey becomes toxic.

Honey won't become toxic, no matter how long you heat it: it does not initially contain any toxic components that are dangerous to humans.

In fact, an organic compound known as 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) is formed when honey is heated. There is an opinion that this compound is highly toxic, that's why it's dangerous to heat honey.

Let's see how toxic this compound is, and whether we risk to get food poisoning from eating a piece of honey cake or adding honey to hot tea.

HMF is an intermediate product of the breakdown of sugars in an acidic medium, which increases with heating, that is, HMF appears in absolutely all products where sugars have undergone heat treatment in the presence of acid.

Therefore, HMF is found in any baked goods, jams, roasted apples and beets, roasted coffee, soda, and often in quantities ten times greater than in honey.

Why, then, is there GOST (State Standard) that regulates the norm of HMF in honey? - let's figure it out in the continuation of the article 😉

## Tip B3-28 — Honey: Myths vs. Reality, Part 2: The GOST HMF Standard Explained

**Source lines:** 3091–3112

**Body:**

part 2

So, we already know that an organic compound 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) is formed when honey is heated and it's neither toxic, nor carcinogenic.

So why are there GOST (State Standard) recommendations according to which the HMF concentration in honey should not exceed 25 mg/kg ?!

The limit for oxy methyl furfural in honey was set not to protect people from a "toxic" product, but to ensure that the product has not undergone overheating during processing.

This indicator is mainly used by honey processors when purchasing lots of honey and helps to determine its age, as well as whether this honey has been overheated during presale processing.

Deviation from the norm characterizes not the danger of heated honey, but rather its uselessness, since enzymes and proteins denaturation takes place much earlier than HMF appears. In this sense, those who like to put honey into hot tea have nothing to worry about, except that honey will lose its medicinal properties.

In any case, after putting a teaspoon of honey into your hot drink, all antioxidants won't disappear at once - they simply won't have time to do it, because your mug is not an autoclave.

Perhaps no one has studied the effect of HMF on human health and its long-term consequences. But taking into account our daily use of a wide variety of products with HMF throughout our lives, this organic compound should not cause serious concerns.

When heated, other organic compounds can form in honey that are harmful to the body (or affect it ambiguously), however, they do not make heated honey noticeably more toxic than almost any other food that you fry or bake.

Moreover, due to the acidic environment (pH 3.5), HMF also appears in raw honey.

In the continuation of the article, we'll analyze why citric acid is sometimes added to the dough for a honey cake and what can be used instead of honey.

## Tip B3-30 — Honey: Myths vs. Reality, Part 4: Adjustments When Substituting & Why Add Citric Acid

**Source lines:** 3151–3183

**Body:**

part 4

#marusya_about_honey

Guys, thank you for being so active under the previous post — I see that the topic of honey is really interesting for you, and therefore I decided not to put off writing the continuation.

🌿 Things to remember when replacing sugar with honey:

🔸 increase baking time as the dough becomes more "heavy" and needs to be baked longer
🔸 lower the temperature by 15 °C, as honey gives the dough a darker color
🔸 reduce the amount of liquid in the recipe; reduce or increase the amount of flour

Summing up, it's definitely possible to replace sugar with honey. However, be sure to stick to the right ratio and, if necessary, make adjustments based on the thickness, consistency and sweetness of the honey used.

🌿Why add citric acid or lemon juice to honey dough?

Adding citric acid is an optional step in the preparation of honey dough and it's just to be on the safe side in case baking soda fails to react properly. If it hasn't activated, you may face several issues:

🔸 smell of baking soda during baking
🔸 bitter aftertaste in cakes
🔸 cake with frosting / berry or fruit filling can be streaked blue — frosting or filling contains acid that reacts with not activated baking soda in the cake layers
🔸 flat, thin crepe-like cake layers

I advise you not to neglect this step, since there is always a possibility that the soda won't react properly for the following reasons:

🔸 different composition of natural honey (more/less acidic, more or less acid in the composition of different types of honey)
🔸 different composition of alkalized cocoa (more/less acidic, more or less acid in the composition of cocoa with varying degrees of alkalization)
🔸 wrong temperature that doesn't ensure a proper reaction between baking soda and acid

💎 Ratio of adding lemon juice to honey dough

1-1.5 g per every 100 g of dough

## Tip B3-32 — Everything You Need to Know About Ganaches, Part 3: Liquid Types & Dosage

**Source lines:** 3228–3266

**Body:**

part 3

#marusya_about_ganache

Apart from chocolate, ganache must contain liquid. But here is where it gets fun: you can use different liquids, e.g:

⚪️ Fresh or pasteurized cream

The fat content of cream should be 35-40%. Thanks to minimal heat treatment, the finished ganache will have a rich creamy taste, but at the same time ‒ a reduced shelf life.

⚪️ Sterilized or UHT cream

The fat content is 35-37%. These types of cream are exposed to high temperatures for a short period of time. This process extends the product's shelf life making these creams chefs' favorites when working with ganaches.

⚪️ Milk

Is used to replace some or all of the cream in ganache. Unsweetened condensed milk (also called evaporated or concentrated milk) is often used to reduce the amount of water added, which negatively affects the shelf life of the ganache.

🟣 Fruit or berry puree

Part of the cream can be replaced with puree. Most often, the ratio of puree and cream is 1:1. It is added to give ganache a fruity flavor and aroma, as well as interesting vivid colors.

💎DOSAGE

▫️milk: maximum 40%
▫️cream: 20-50%
▫️puree: maximum 50%

How the liquid added affects the ganache?

🟣 TEXTURE
◽️ gives the ganache a silky, melting texture during tasting (provided the fat content is at least 35%)
◽️ in some cases, e.g. when preparing whipped ganache, cream is a necessary element for increasing the volume, as well as trapping and stabilizing air bubbles in the finished ganache

🟣 MOISTURE CONTENT
◽️ liquid with a water content of about 60% dissolves sugar and makes the chocolate less dense

🟣 TASTE
▫️ due to the fat molecules present in the composition, it has aroma-retaining properties and is a carrier of taste to our receptors

## Tip B3-36 — Why Do You Need to Sift the Flour?

**Source lines:** 3377–3399

**Body:**

WHY DO YOU NEED TO SIFT THE FLOUR?

Almost every pastry recipe calls for sifted flour. Have you ever thought about why you should sift it?

Let's start with what we have actually moved away from for a long time, thanks to modern production technologies.

In the past, it was always necessary to sift the flour in order to:

▪️ get rid of weevils and other bugs
⚠️Nowadays contaminated foods should be disposed immediately.⚠️
▪️ get rid of the smallest pieces of stone for grinding grain — quern. The saddle quern consisted of a flat stone bed and a rounded stone to be operated manually against it; later, millstones developed from those hand-operated querns
▪️ get rid of debris — threads from packing bags, cereals from a neighboring box, pieces of paper or plastic bags, etc.

Nowadays, here are a few times you should sift your flour for baking:

▪️ to eliminate and prevent lumps — when your flour has been sitting around for a while, seems to be tightly packed, or has been stored at high humidity, lumps form even in high-quality flour.
▪️ to get a homogeneous, uniform base for the dough — sifted flour mixes better with other ingredients, and the dough behaves more predictably
▪️ to get much lighter and airier flour — it's especially important when baking sponges
▪️ to guarantee you get the right amount when the recipe uses cups instead of grams as a unit of measure (American recipes) — sifted flour will have a larger volume than not sifted

💡 Lifehack from Marusya

If the flour you are using is fairly fresh and does not contain any lumps, in most cases you can skip sifting, and make your flour airier simply by mixing it with a whisk.

## Tip B3-37 — Honey Cake Layers: Factors That Affect the Intensity of Honey Taste and Aroma

**Source lines:** 3400–3418

**Body:**

HONEY CAKE LAYERS: FACTORS THAT AFFECT THE INTENSITY OF HONEY TASTE AND AROMA

Honey cake layers can have a different intensity of honey taste and aroma: from delicate, subtle honey notes to strong, pronounced ones. What does it depend on?

The very first and seemingly obvious answer that comes to mind is the amount of honey added. But in fact, this is far from the primary factor.

1️⃣ The intensity of the honey taste and aroma in the cake layers depends primarily on the heat treatment the batter undergoes before baking — the more you heat the honey mixture, the more pronounced taste will have the cake layers:

🔸subtle honey taste and aroma can be obtained by bringing the mixture for the honey dough to 60–70 °C over a water bath or in a saucepan
🔸 moderately pronounced taste is obtained by bringing the honey or the honey mixture to a boil (100–116 °C)
🔸 rich, pronounced honey taste and aroma of the cake layers is achieved by boiling the honey mixture or by using the dough made with burnt honey (> 116 °C)

2️⃣ Secondly, the intensity of the honey taste and aroma of the cake layers depends on the type of honey. If you want to get cakes with a light, delicate honey taste, opt for light types of honey, for example, acacia or linden. Buckwheat honey and other dark types should be preferred if you like cakes with a rich, pronounced honey taste.

3️⃣ The amount of honey added to the dough makes less difference to the intensity of honey taste and aroma of cake layers compared to the type of honey and its heat treatment. Therefore, do not be surprised that the amount of honey in honey recipes can vary from 60 to 450 g, depending on the recipe.

## Tip B3-38 — Brown vs. White Eggs — Is There a Difference?

**Source lines:** 3419–3443

**Body:**

BROWN VS. WHITE EGGS — IS THERE A DIFFERENCE?

#marusya_theory_tips

Guys, you probably noticed that in some countries in supermarkets the prices for brown and white eggs differ? Is there any reason for that? Or is it just a marketing ploy?

Let's figure it out. But first things first.

Is there really a difference between brown and white eggs?

✔️ Yes — in color

Eggs come in many colors, depending on the breed of the hen. Chickens have different pigment genes that determine the color of the egg shell. Generally speaking, white shell eggs come from hens with white feathers and white earlobes, while brown shell eggs are produced by hens with brown feathers and red earlobes.
📍some chickens have a unique pigment gene and lay multi-colored eggs — light blue, green and even pink

✖️ No — in terms of nutritional value and taste

The color of the eggshell is not indicative of the best quality of the egg. In fact, there is no distinguishable difference between brown and white eggs when it comes to taste and nutritional value.
📍The nutritional value and palatability of an egg depend solely on the diet and environment of the chicken. The food eaten by the hen can directly affect how an egg tastes, which means that pasture-raised hens usually lay eggs that have a richer taste and higher concentration of useful macro- and microelements

❓ Is the higher price for brown eggs a marketing ploy?

✖️No — hens with brown feathers tend to be larger than hens with white feathers, meaning they consume more feed. Therefore, brown eggs need to be sold at a higher price to make up for the extra costs

✔️ Yes — if you have faced an unscrupulous dealer speculating on their more "natural" appearance
