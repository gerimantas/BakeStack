### Tip B1-01 — Flavor Infusion: What, With What, and How (Parts 1–3, incl. Cold & Hot Infusion methods)
**Source lines:** 1–111

Body:

FLAVOR INFUSION: WHAT, WITH WHAT, AND HOW

At first glance, this topic may seem very simple, as if there is not much to discuss. But! You might be surprised to learn that even when I was running a masterclass at a large-scale production facility, my recommendation to switch to cold aromatization came as a surprise to the team.

In reality, aroma and taste are inseparably connected and should always be considered as a whole.

Flavor–aroma pairing is a fascinating field you inevitably encounter when working in pastry arts. Once baking basic shortbread cookies or classic apple cakes is no longer inspiring, a natural desire arises to explore new or unconventional flavor combinations and to introduce subtle aromatic notes.

A well-chosen aroma and the dessert's aftertaste can calm a person or, on the contrary, make them feel more energized; it can bring comfort or encourage reflection. For example, vanilla conveys warmth and a sense of calm, cinnamon evokes the feeling of a cozy autumn, while lemongrass and lavender have soothing properties.

To transfer aroma from one ingredient to another, they must be combined under specific conditions:

🔸 mixed together (stirred or blended)
🔸 chilled together
🔸 heated together
🔸 simmered together

What can be aromatized?
In mousses:

🔺 the liquid base (cream / milk)
🔺 the sugar syrup used for whipping egg whites (if the mousse is based on Italian meringue)
🔺 mousses may also be aromatized using pastes

📍 Only thick pastes are used — those that do not compromise, but rather improve the dessert's texture:
— nut pastes, praline paste, certain fruit pastes

In ganaches:

🔺 cream
🔺 fruit purée
🔺 water

In berry fillings:

🔺 the berries themselves or berry purée are aromatized using the cold method or through vacuum infusion
In sponge cakes and other baked goods exposed to high temperatures:
🔺 the aromatizing ingredient is added directly to the recipe

In the previous part, we discussed WHAT can be flavored. Today, let's talk about WHAT TO USE to do that.

Most likely, the first thing that comes to mind is flavorings. Both natural or nature-identical flavorings, as well as synthetic ones, can help add aroma to desserts.

This method has 3 obvious advantages:

1️⃣ it doesn't require much time or effort — add a few drops, stir, and it's done;

2️⃣ it helps in situations where you aren't allowed to add extra ingredients in order not to change the texture;

3️⃣ it's convenient when you need an unusual flavor (bubble gum, California strawberry, tiramisu).

In addition to flavorings, you can enhance the aroma of your desserts with:

🔸 vanilla (pods / paste / extract)
🔸 spices and seasonings (pepper, cinnamon, cardamom, anise, coriander, star anise, cloves, ginger)
🔸 herbs and flowers (tarragon, basil, dill, thyme, rosemary, lavender)
🔸 tea (black, matcha, red, green)
🔸 fruit pastes
🔸 citrus zest
🔸 freeze-dried berries
🔸 tonka beans
🔸 coffee beans
🔸 alcoholic beverages (rum, brandy, liqueurs, infusions)
🔸 syrups
🔸 natural essential extracts and oils

And this list is far from complete!

We've figured out WHAT to use. Now let's talk about HOW to use it. There are several flavoring and infusion methods:

🔺 cold infusion
🔺 hot infusion
🔺 flavoring by simmering
🔺 vacuum sealing

COLD INFUSION

Cold infusion involves adding a specific flavoring component (spices, herbs, zest, etc.) to a liquid (cream, milk, berry purée) and then infusing it in the refrigerator for at least 12 hours.

This is the longest method — flavor develops slowly and is less intense than when using the hot infusion method.
To completely extract aroma from fresh herbs, you'll need 3–5 days, and from dried ones — about 20 days.

Continuing to explore the topic of flavor infusion, today we'll look at the hot method, which I use quite often when developing recipes.
THE HOT METHOD

Many of you have probably already guessed that the hot method involves heat treatment—and you're absolutely right.

Hot infusion means bringing a liquid to a boil together with spices, herbs, or other flavoring ingredients, and then letting it steep for 30–60 minutes.

You can use cream, milk, or berry purée as the base for infusion. For example, you can infuse cream with dried lavender flowers, as I did in the Blueberry–Lavender Cheesecake, or create a rich coffee aroma by infusing cream with ground coffee (the Coffee–Baileys Cheesecake).

It's very simple. But there are a few secrets I'd love to share with you.

SECRETS OF THE HOT METHOD

📌 Heat the liquid for infusion over medium or low heat so it warms up gradually.

📌 During the infusion process, try to use as little extra equipment as possible when preparing ingredients. For example, it's better to zest citrus directly into the cream rather than onto a board first and then transferring it. Spices, herbs, and citrus contain a large amount of essential oils, which evaporate easily.

📌 Once the liquid reaches a boil, remove the saucepan from the heat immediately. At excessively high temperatures, flavoring ingredients may release compounds that negatively affect the taste and aroma.

📌 After heating, it's best to cover the liquid with plastic wrap directly on the surface. If you use a lid, excess condensation may form and drip back into the liquid, and a film can develop on the surface that you'll later have to remove.

📌 After the infusion time, strain the liquid through a sieve or cheesecloth.

📌 Once completely cooled, the infused liquid can be stored in the refrigerator for the same shelf life as the base product. However, keep in mind that the aroma will gradually fade with each day of storage.

### Tip B1-02 — Factors That Affect Starch Functionality
**Source lines:** 112–128

Body:

FACTORS THAT AFFECT STARCH FUNCTIONALITY

In previous posts, we discussed different types of starch and how starch works. Today, let's focus on what specifically affects the rate of starch thickening.

📚 So, the factors that influence the gelatinization temperature (t°) of starch include:

— TYPE OF STARCH — each type of starch has an optimal amount of heat required for proper gelatinization.

The time required for complete gelatinization of root starches is always shorter than that required for corn starch. In most cases, unmodified root starches should not be brought to a boil. When cooked for too long, unmodified root starches develop an excessively elastic or stringy texture.

— AMOUNT OF TENDERIZERS — sugars and fats slow down the rate at which starch granules absorb water and swell.

The slower they absorb water, the longer it takes for starch granules to gelatinize. In fact, if enough sugar is present, it can completely prevent starch gelatinization. This is one of the ways sugars and fats tenderize baked goods: they reduce the amount of gelatinized starch, which is responsible for structure formation. Sugar also increases the transparency of starch-thickened mixtures.

— AMOUNT OF ACID — acids hydrolyze (break down) large starch molecules into smaller ones, reducing their thickening power.

Acids also weaken starch granules, allowing them to gelatinize faster and more easily. In fact, if enough acid is present, gelatinization occurs so rapidly that the starch mixture may appear not to thicken at all.

### Tip B1-03 — Starch: How Does It Work?
**Source lines:** 129–157

Body:

STARCH. How does it work?

The process depends on the temperature setting. When starch granules are placed in cold water, its molecules attract water molecules and the granules swell just slightly. However, when the water is heated, the starch granules undergo an irreversible process called GELATINIZATION.

There is an optimal amount of heat for proper thickening and gel formation:

— too little heat → too few granules swell

— too much heat → too many granules break down

Undercooking or overcooking starch results in insufficient thickening and gel formation.

UNDERCOOKING starch causes additional problems as well:

— because raw granules are hard and dense, undercooked starch feels gritty in the mouth

— undercooked starch is opaque and has an unpleasant starch taste

— if stored for a day or longer, undercooked starch mixtures tend to release droplets or even pools of water around the gel

There are many factors affect the gelatinization t° of starches. The higher the gelatinization t°, the longer starch takes to gelatinize and, thus, the greater the risk of undercooking.

Here are the factors that affect starch gelatinization t°:

• type of starch used

• amount of fats and sugar

• amount of acid

### Tip B1-04 — All About Starch: Types and Uses
**Source lines:** 158–187

Body:

ALL ABOUT STARCH: TYPES AND USES

A starch molecule consists of two polysaccharides—amylose and amylopectin, which are themselves made of glucose.

Different types of starch have unique properties. There are four main types of starch:
⁃ Grain starches (corn, rice, wheat, waxy corn)
⁃ Root starches (potato, tapioca)
⁃ Modified starches
⁃ Instant (quick-action) starches

All starches start as either grain or root starches. Instant and modified starches are made from these by chemical treatment or physical processing.

ROOT starches differ from GRAIN starches because they contain fewer amylose molecules and more amylopectin:
⁃ they don't have a grainy taste
⁃ they have better clarity
⁃ they form a softer gel

MODIFIED starches are designer starches created by manufacturers to achieve specific desired properties:
⁃ increased resistance to excessive heat and acid, which usually thins products thickened with starch
⁃ better stability during freezing, which usually causes starch gels to shrink, stick together, or spread
⁃ to change texture
⁃ to speed up or slow down gelatinization

However, the primary reason for using modified starch is its enhanced stability.

INSTANT starches thicken and form gels without heating. To make instant starch, the manufacturer pre-cooks (gelatinizes) it and then dries it, so the granules absorb water without heat.

They are perfect for thickening heat-sensitive products. For example, the bright green color and delicate flavor of kiwi balls are preserved when thickened with instant starch.

### Tip B1-05 — Thickening and Gel Formation: What's the Difference?
**Source lines:** 188–217

Body:

THICKENING and GEL FORMATION. What's the difference?

We all use starch and gelatin to make desserts, but few people really know how they work.

Let's break it down.

Thickeners and gelling agents (thick. & gell.) work by absorbing or holding a large amount of water. Fun fact: the most common thick. & gell. in baking is a plain old egg! 🥚

Thick. & gell. — like gelatin, plant gums, and starches — all have one thing in common: they're made of very large molecules. Some, like starches and gums, are polysaccharides. Others, like gelatin, are proteins.

POLYSACCHARIDES are giant molecules made of many sugar units (saccharides) linked together.

PROTEINS are giant molecules made of many amino acids linked together.

THICKENING happens when water and other molecules or particles in your recipe move very slowly.

This happens when:
⁃ Large molecules touch each other and tangle freely
⁃ Water is absorbed and trapped by swollen starch granules
⁃ Air bubbles (in foams) or fat droplets (in emulsions) slow down water movement

GEL FORMATION occurs when water and other molecules in the recipe cannot move at all.

This happens when large molecules bind tightly or tangle into a network that traps water and other substances.

Some gelling agents (like agar-agar) are so powerful that a gel can form even when water makes up over 99% of the product!

Some ingredients can do both — thicken in small amounts and form gels in larger amounts — like gelatin, corn starch, and pectin.

Other ingredients only thicken. They never form gels, no matter how much you add. They simply make the mixture thicker and stickier — similar to guar gum or waxy cornstarch.

**Notes:** Standalone tip with clear title and closed scope (conceptual distinction, not specific to one starch type).

### Tip B1-06 — Shelf Life of Frostings
**Source lines:** 218–252

Body:

SHELF LIFE OF FROSTINGS

✅ Preparing frostings

I constantly remind my students that making frosting is a process inseparable from assembling the cake.

All whipped (aerated) frostings — buttercream, ganache, sour-cream or cream-cheese frosting — must be used right after preparation.

‼️ They cannot be stored and shouldn't be prepared in advance.

Butter-based frostings can be kept in the fridge at 2–6°C (~35–43°F) for no more than 5 hours.

✅ Storing finished products that contain frostings

The container must be clean, dry, and free of foreign odors.

All baked goods must be stored in the fridge at 2–6°C (~35–43°F) after being made.

The shelf life of frostings depends on their composition (eggs, alcohol, purées, fat content of dairy products, etc.) and storage conditions (room-temp display, fridge, and how often it is opened).

Buttercream frosting: 24 to 72 hours depending on ingredients (24 hours if eggs, like "Charlotte").

Buttercream can be frozen for up to 1 month.

Meringue frosting: up to 72 hours in the fridge, up to 12 hours at room temp (~20–22°C/~68–72°F).

Custard-based frosting: at 4–6°C (~39–43°F) it keeps 48–72 hours (2–3 days). At room temp (~23–50°C/~73–122°F) only 6–12 hours because it spoils quickly. Cool quickly to 4°C (~39°F) after cooking to prevent bacterial growth, especially in the 23–50°C (~73–122°F) range.

Sour-cream frosting: in the fridge 4–6°C (~39–43°F) - 12–36 hours depending on fat content and ingredients. At room temp (~23–50°C/~73–122°F) - up to 6 hours. If used in cakes, keeps up to 3 days in the fridge at 4–6°C (~39–43°F).

Cream-based frosting: depends on ingredients and preparation method. Cream-cheese, sour-cream-and-cream, and butter-and-cream frostings keep up to 36 hours in the fridge at 4–6°C (~39–43°F).

Cheesecake-style or cream-cheese frosting: up to 48 hours in the fridge at 4–6°C (~39–43°F). Cream cheese should not sit at room temp (~23–50°C/~73–122°F) for more than 2 hours.

Ganache: depending on composition, from 72 hours in the fridge 4–6°C (~39–43°F) to several months at room temp (~23–50°C/~73–122°F).

### Tip B1-07 — Mascarpone vs. Cream Cheese: Key Differences
**Source lines:** 253–283

Body:

Mascarpone vs. Cream Cheese: Key Differences

Despite their similarities and wide use in cooking, mascarpone and cream cheese differ in production methods, composition, and properties.

Understanding these differences helps you choose the right product each time and substitute it when needed.

Mascarpone

🔸Origin: traditional Italian cheese
🔸Fat content: 70–75%, almost as rich as butter
🔸Flavor and aroma: delicate, creamy, with a buttery note; almost neutral, enhancing other flavors harmoniously
🔸Texture: very soft, silky, prone to separating if overwhipped
🔸Production: typically made by adding acid to warmed heavy cream. The combination of acid and heat coagulates the casein, forming a fine, smooth cheese mass
🔸Uses: classic ingredient in tiramisu; used in mousses, creams, and desserts where high fat content and velvety texture are desired

Cream Cheese

🔸Origin: industrially developed product, common in the US and Europe
🔸Fat content: on average 30–35%
🔸Flavor and aroma: mild with noticeable tang and slight saltiness, which helps balance the sweetness in desserts
🔸Texture: denser and more stable compared to mascarpone
🔸Production: made by adding lactic acid bacteria and often enzymes to milk or cream. Once the liquid whey is drained, the cheese is processed until it reaches the desired consistency
🔸Uses: base for cheesecakes, frostings, and fillings; provides structure and holds its shape well

Mascarpone offers a rich flavor, creaminess, and a buttery texture, but is less stable during processing.

Cream cheese ensures density and structure, adds a light tang that balances sweetness, but is less intensely creamy.

To sum up, the choice depends on the desired result:
• Mascarpone is ideal for delicate frostings and mousse-based desserts
• Cream cheese is best for recipes where stability, shape, and flavor balance are important👌

### Tip B1-08 — Why Baking Soda ≠ Baking Powder?
**Source lines:** 284–316

Body:

Why baking soda ≠ baking powder? 🤔

And how to know which one you need for what you're baking?

✔️Baking soda

Works only in combination with an acid—kefir, lemon juice, honey, etc.

The reaction starts immediately as soon as the ingredients are combined. Carbon dioxide begins to release, so the dough must go straight into the oven. If you wait too long, the baked goods won't rise properly.

✔️Baking powder

It's a combination of baking soda and a dry acid.

It comes in several forms, the most common being double-acting. Baked goods made with double-acting baking powder need heat to fully activate the leavening. A small amount of carbon dioxide is released when mixing; the rest is released during baking. This removes the need for rushing to portion and bake to ensure the dough rises just as good.

✔️Ammonium (ammonium carbonate)

Lesser known, but a very effective leavening agent.

Perfect for thin and dry baked goods— various cookies, including gingerbread cookies, wafers. Gives incredible crispness and lightness. However, it's not used in large cakes or loaves: the smell of ammonia can stay in the finished product.

❗️Substitution

•Baking soda for baking powder—1 : 3 or triple the amount of baking powder.

•Baking powder for baking soda—3 : 1 or use 1 part soda + 2 parts cream of tartar for every 3 parts baking powder.

👉So if you're making sponges or cakes with acidic ingredients, you can use baking soda.

👉If you need more control and stability—choose baking powder or a combo of soda + baking powder.

👉For thin crispy cookies or gingerbread dough—ammonium will be your secret ingredient.

### Tip B1-09 — Why Does Crème Anglaise Turn Out Silky vs. Curdled?
**Source lines:** 317–350

Body:

Why does crème anglaise turn out silky and delicate for some, but curdled and eggy for others?
👉 And which cooking method is the safest?

Crème anglaise—also known as vanilla custard sauce—serves as the base for many desserts, including Bavarian creams, ice cream, and crèmeux. It's made from milk, sugar, and egg yolks that are gently heated until lightly thickened.

⚡ Here precision is key: the temperature should not exceed about 82°C (~180°F), otherwise the yolks will scramble. Always cook slowly and attentively.

💡 Technically, it can be cooked up to 85°C (~185°F) without curdling, but it's safer to stop slightly earlier, as the sauce continues to heat even after being removed from the stove due to the residual heat in the pan.

🥄 Main tips for success:

Whisk yolks with sugar right away—if you let them sit, the sugar will draw out moisture, leaving lumps of dried yolk.

Heat the milk almost to boiling so the process goes faster.

Temper the yolks by slowly pouring in the hot milk in a thin stream while whisking constantly.

Cook only over low heat, stirring continuously.

Test doneness in two ways:
• With a thermometer—82°C (~180°F).
• By the spoon test—the sauce should lightly coat the back of a spoon instead of running off like milk.

Keep in mind this is a very light sauce, so don't expect it to thicken too much.

Immediately strain the sauce into a bowl set over an ice bath to cool quickly. Stir occasionally to ensure even cooling.

💡 If the sauce curdles, sometimes you can rescue it. Quickly add 30–60 ml (2–4 tbsp) of cold milk, transfer to a blender, and blend on high speed until smooth again.

Cooking methods:

▶️ Steaming—slowest, but the safest method.
▶️ Bowl in simmering water—a balance of speed and control.
▶️ Direct heat—best for experienced chefs, since the risk of ending up with scrambled eggs is much higher.

### Tip B1-10 — Butter: The Ideal (But Tricky) Fat
**Source lines:** 351–379

Body:

Butter — the ideal fat for any baked goods, but along with its advantages, it also comes with some obvious drawbacks.

Let's break it down.

Advantages of butter:
• Pleasant mouthfeel;
• Rich, creamy flavor.

Drawbacks of butter:
• Expensive — several times pricier than alternative fats;
• One of the trickiest fats to work with due to its narrow plasticity range—too hard to work with straight from the fridge, but it softens and melts very quickly in a warm room.

❗️The best temperature for creaming butter lies in a very narrow window: 18–21°C (~64–70°F).

• Spoils faster than other fats, especially unsalted butter. It's highly prone to bacterial spoilage if not kept refrigerated for short storage or frozen for longer storage. Spoiled butter tastes sour, like bad milk, or develops a sharp rancid flavor.

One of the trickiest parts of working with butter—especially for beginners—is finding the right consistency for creaming.

❗️Cold butter (below 16°C/~60°F) is too firm to trap nice, even air pockets, while overly warm butter (above 24°C/~75°F) is too soft.

However, just as important as the butter temperature is, the room temperature is also crucial.

In summer, even if you start with butter at the perfect (~21°C/~70°F), after just a few minutes of mixing, the warmth of the bowl and additional friction heat can quickly raise the butter's temperature to 24–25°C (~75–77°F). At that point, it creams very poorly.

❗️That's why in summer, I always begin creaming butter at no higher than 16–17°C (~60–63°F).

Also, pay attention to mixing speed—it should be a moderate (medium) speed.

If you whip on high speed, the friction heats the ingredients too much. You'll trap fewer air pockets, and the ones that do form will often be too large and uneven.

### Tip B1-11 — Sugar: Caramelization and the Maillard Reaction
**Source lines:** 380–403

Body:

Sugar: Caramelization and the Maillard reaction—what they are and how they differ

In the culinary world, these two chemical processes play a key role in creating the color, aroma, and taste of many desserts. They are similar, but have important differences that every professional should understand ⬇️

Caramelization is the process of heating sugar, during which it melts, darkens, and develops a characteristic sweet, nutty flavor and aroma. It is a chemical reaction that occurs at high temperatures (over 160–170°C), when sugar breaks down into different compounds, creating new flavors and colors.

The Maillard reaction is a chemical interaction between sugars and amino acids in proteins (for example, from flour, eggs, milk), which starts at lower temperatures and produces more complex roasted flavors such as cocoa, coffee, and nuts.

Even a small amount of protein greatly accelerates this reaction, which is why baked goods with dairy products and eggs brown faster.

The Maillard reaction can occur slowly even at room temperature — for example, in powdered milk or white chocolate, which change color and taste over time.

❕ A few more important points:

• Monosaccharides (fructose, glucose) brown faster than disaccharides (sucrose), so invert syrup speeds up browning.

• Among sweeteners, the browning speed usually goes (from fastest to slowest):
fructose > glucose > lactose > maltose > sucrose > isomalt.

• Minerals such as copper and iron, even in trace amounts, significantly enhance browning. They are present in some water, unrefined syrups (molasses, honey, maple syrup), and salt.

• The pH of the environment also matters: alkalis (such as baking soda) raise pH and increase browning, while acids (kefir, sour cream, cream of tartar) slow it down.

• Higher baking temperatures intensify browning, which is why the crust on bread or sponge cake is always darker than the inside.

### Tip B1-12 — Sugar: How It Works in Batter
**Source lines:** 404–449

Body:

Sugar: How It Works in Batter

All sugars are hygroscopic, meaning they have the ability to attract and retain water.

That's why sugar plays a key role in:
✔️creating a tender texture
✔️retaining moisture
✔️extending shelf life

CREATING TENDER TEXTURES

Once dissolved, sugar prevents:
• gluten formation
• protein coagulation
• starch gelatinization

In other words, sugar slows down structure formation — and because of that, the batter has more time in the oven to rise, and the baked good remains soft and tender after baking.

Why does this happen?

Because gluten, proteins, and starch need water to form structure, and sugar — as a hygroscopic substance — absorbs and binds water, leaving less available liquid for the structure-builders.

Sugar raises the temperature at which proteins coagulate and starch gelatinizes, which delays structure formation.

👉 As a result, the more sugar in a recipe, the more tender the baked good becomes.

❕BUT it's important to find the right balance

▪ If there's too much sugar, structure barely forms: the product either doesn't rise, or rises — and quickly collapses when cooled.

▪ If there's too little sugar, structure forms too early — before steam has time to expand the dough. The baked good ends up with poor volume, and its surface tears because steam breaks through the prematurely formed crust.

RETAINING MOISTURE AND EXTENDING SHELF LIFE

Sugar's hygroscopicity is the main reason why baked goods stay soft even after a few days.

🔹 The most effective for this is fructose — it retains moisture better than any other sugar.

High-fructose syrups also work well, including:

– invert syrup
– honey
– molasses
– agave syrup

They retain moisture better than regular granulated sugar and are often used in recipes where lasting freshness is important.

### Tip B1-13 — Hot Cream & Cool Nerves: Everything About Caramel
**Source lines:** 450–473

Body:

Hot Cream & Cool Nerves: Everything You Need To Know About Caramel 👌

Caramel can be temperamental, but it's not nearly as scary once you know a few important things. All it really "asks" for is attention, consistency, and a little respect for physics!

❕ Here are some essential tips 👇

• To avoid crystallization, glucose syrup is often used—about 10–20% of the sugar amount. It helps keep the mixture smooth. If you don't have syrup, it's very important that your cookware is perfectly clean.

• Another critical factor is the heavy whipping cream. It must be hot. Cold cream coming into contact with dry caramel causes a shock reaction: the mixture seizes, splatters, and forms lumps. To avoid this, add very hot cream gradually, in small portions, while constantly stirring.

• In large-batch recipes, it's helpful to divide the sugar into several parts. For example, melt part of it first, then gradually add the rest. This helps better control color, temperature, and prevents uneven melting.

• Caramel can be made in two ways:

dry (no liquid added)

or wet (with a small amount of water).
If you opt for the wet method, remember: do not stir the syrup, as this may trigger crystallization.

• It's best to add the butter once the caramel has cooled slightly (to about 45–50°C/113-122°F). This reduces the risk of splitting and gives you a smooth, silky texture with no oil on the surface and a gentler, creamy flavor.

Egg whites straight from the fridge don't whip well.

⏩Salt

⏩Whisk type
Thick wires—create large bubbles, less stable foam.

### Tip B1-16 — Factors Affecting Meringue Stability: Fats & Acids
**Source lines:** 548–573

Body:

Factors Affecting Meringue Stability:

❕FATS & ACIDS

LIPIDS
Everyone knows that if even a drop of yolk gets into your egg whites, it's best not to whip them into meringue.

But do you know why? What exactly does that yolk do that even beginner bakers are well aware it's the sworn enemy of perfect meringue?

Let's break it down⬇️

Lipids — fats, oils, and emulsifiers — interfere with aeration. Depending on their type and amount, lipids can either slow down or completely prevent aeration.

This is especially true for the lipids found in egg yolks, particularly lecithin. Even a small amount of yolk can prevent egg whites from whipping properly.

Lipids disrupt aeration by coating the proteins, thus preventing them from unfolding and bonding. But they do more than that — lipids compete with proteins for space on the surface of air bubbles. Since lipids on their own can't form a strong, cohesive, and elastic network like egg whites can, the bubbles they coat expand quickly and then collapse.

PRO TIP
To avoid problems with fats, oils, or emulsifiers when making meringue, always use a clean bowl, carefully separate every trace of yolk from the whites, and avoid plastic bowls and utensils — they tend to absorb grease and are difficult to fully clean.

ACID

Acid helps stabilize the meringue by lowering the pH. Cream of tartar is the most commonly used acid, but lemon juice or vinegar can also stabilize the foam.

Add acid early in the whipping process — it may take a bit longer to whip, but the resulting protein network will be more elastic and resistant to overwhipping, folding, piping, and baking. The meringue will also be whiter.

### Tip B1-18 — How Liquid Egg Whites Turn Into Foam During Whipping
**Source lines:** 633–641

Body:

Have you ever wondered how liquid egg whites turn into foam during whipping?

When you whip eggs, two things happen simultaneously.

Air bubbles are beaten into the liquid, and the proteins in the egg whites denature—or unfold. The unfolded proteins move quickly through the liquid to the surface of the air bubbles.

Once there, neighboring proteins bond—or aggregate—around the bubbles, forming a film-like network. Surrounded by these strong, flexible films, the air bubbles are less likely to burst, allowing more bubbles to be beaten in.

What happens to egg white proteins during whipping is similar, though not identical, to what occurs with them during heating. In both cases, protein molecules unfold and bond, forming a type of structure.

### Tip B1-21 — A Whisk, a Paddle, or a Dough Hook? (Parts 1–3, incl. Creaming Method)

Body:

A WHISK, A PADDLE, OR A DOUGH HOOK?
At first glance, the answer seems simple: ✔️ A whisk for whipping ✔️ A paddle for mixing soft dough or batter ✔️ A dough hook for kneading stiff dough

But in practice, as I've observed, these distinctions often confuse students. Choosing the correct attachment isn't always straightforward!

For example, should you use a whisk or a paddle attachment when creaming butter for a sponge cake or cupcake?

What about making frosting or shortbread dough?

Is a whisk better when mixing the cheese filling for a cheesecake, or should you opt for a paddle attachment? Perhaps a paddle with flexible silicone edges?

And what if you're using a hand mixer—does that change the choice entirely?😳

Let's break it down entirely because selecting the proper attachment is crucial to achieving perfect desserts.

WHISK

The whisk is specifically designed for whipping, a process where the goal is to incorporate as many tiny air bubbles as possible into the mixture.

You'll need a whisk for tasks like whipping whole eggs for sponge cakes, beating egg whites for meringue, preparing egg yolks for sponge cake or frosting, whipping cream or any cream-based frosting, preparing marshmallows, mousse, or soufflés with gelatin.

Seems straightforward, right? But here's a twist…

Have you ever noticed that some Kenwood whisks have thin rods while others have thicker ones? 🤔

Yes, the thickness of your whisk's rods truly matters. A whisk with thick rods cannot whip meringue or cream to achieve the correct consistency.

Foams whipped with thick rods will have large, uneven, and unstable air bubbles. This results in sponge cakes with poor volume and mousses that lack tenderness and airiness.

Therefore, if your stand mixer only comes with a whisk with thick rods, I recommend purchasing one with thin rods separately. Until then, you'll need to whip meringues and cream using a hand mixer.

So, what is a whisk with thick rods good for? It's perfect for preparing frostings made with cream cheese or mascarpone combined with heavy whipped cream.

PADDLE – This attachment is used for mixing batters, soft doughs and for creaming butter.

Yes, creaming! While we often use the term "whipping," in baking literature, butter is always "creamed," not whipped.

First of all, I want to sincerely thank you all for the incredible engagement on the previous post. Your comments and ❤️s truly inspire me to keep sharing more valuable content with you.

Today, let's talk about the CREAMING METHOD—a fundamental technique in baking that involves softening a solid fat, such as butter or shortening, into a smooth, homogeneous mass before combining it with other ingredients. This method is commonly used when making buttercream, cake and cupcake batters, and cookie dough.

During creaming, fat is whipped together with sugar/dry ingredients until the mixture becomes light and fluffy, and increases in volume. This happens because tiny air bubbles are incorporated into the mass and retained within the semi-solid fat. These air bubbles remain in the batter and expand during baking, contributing to the leavening and fluffy texture of the final product.

So, if our goal is to introduce air into the butter—just like we do when whipping cream—why don't we use a whisk?

The answer lies in the nature of the ingredients. A whisk is perfect for incorporating air into liquid ingredients like eggs or cream. Butter, however, is best creamed while it's in a solid but pliable (plastic) state.

If you try using a whisk, it will likely become clogged with butter, causing the mixer to work "idle". That's why a paddle attachment is more suitable for creaming butter.

But you might rightfully point out that a paddle attachment can also just smear the butter along the sides of the bowl, causing the mixer to run idle as well.

And you'd be absolutely right—especially when working with a small amount of butter and using a paddle that doesn't have flexible silicone edges. In that case, you'll often need to stop the mixer periodically to scrape the butter off the sides and bottom of the bowl manually.

This is where the paddle attachment with soft silicone edges becomes a game changer…

### Tip B1-31 — Flour Theory: Protein, Starch, and Damaged Starch Granules
**Source lines:** 939–957

Body:

Have been delving into the theory of flour for a while.

I have known about the power and importance of gluten (protein) for quite some time. But this time it's a completely different level.

In nutshell. In addition to protein (~12%), the main part of flour is starch (~71%).

On which type of wheat for making flour is used (hard or soft kernel) will depend not only the quantity and quality of protein, the texture of the flour, but also the number of broken and damaged starch granules.

That is, the harder the wheat kernel, the more difficult it is to grind it into fine flour and the more starch granules will be damaged in the process.

And damaged starch granules absorb much more water than intact ones, which slows staling.

Damaged granules are also more susceptible than intact ones to breakdown down by amylase, which further slows staling.

In addition, as the amylase breaks down starches into sugars, yeast fermentation can increase.

That's why flour for bread is milled from hard red wheat, which not only has a high protein content, but also many damaged starch granules.

Bread flours are typically used for pan breads, rolls, croissants and sweet yeast doughs.

### Tip B1-34 — 4 Primary Ways in Which Fat Contributes to Leavening
**Source lines:** 1000–1016

Body:

4 PRIMARY WAYS IN WHICH FAT CONTRIBUTES TO LEAVENING

1️⃣ All plastic fats contain tiny air bubbles, and some fats (such as butter and margarine) contain droplets of water.

The real leavening agents are air and water (steam), while fat serves an auxiliary function by helping trap and preserve air within the dough.

2️⃣ Loosening of puff pastry

Solid fat layers create cavities in the dough upon melting, subsequently filled by hot steam. As the steam expands due to heat, it pushes the layers of dough apart.

3️⃣ During the whipping process, additional air bubbles get into the plastic fats.

The air bubbles are surrounded and protected by numerous tiny, hard fat crystals that prevent them from bursting.

4️⃣ Emulsifiers have the ability to trap and retain large volumes of air, especially when using high-ratio shortenings.

For instance, during the whipping process of plastic shortening, air is trapped. With the help of an emulsifier, it is first retained in the fat and then in the dough in the form of very small, strong bubbles evenly distributed throughout the dough.

### Tip B1-38 — The Physics of Flaky Puff Pastry Formation During Baking
**Source lines:** 1097–1124

Body:

THE PHYSICS OF FLAKY PUFF PASTRY FORMATION DURING BAKING

Puff pastry consists of multiple, invisible to the eye, but intact layers of dough, separated from each other by equally thin and intact layers of pliable fat.

As the oven temperature rises, the layers of fat gradually melt. Simultaneously, water within the fat transforms into steam, expanding as the temperature ascends. This steam fills the voids left by the melting fat.

The melted fat acts as a barrier, delaying the escape of steam from the dough. Consequently, under the pressure of the expanding steam, the layers of dough puff up, creating the characteristic flakiness. Meanwhile, the lipids in the fat cause the dough to fry, resulting in a delectably golden-brown and crispy exterior.

Towards the end of the baking process, the dough stabilizes, yielding a final product with numerous delicate layers.

⚠️ The rising of baked goods occurs in the interstices between the layers of dough; however, the individual layers of dough themselves remain flat.

Hence, in areas where the layers of dough make contact, there is minimal flakiness and rising.

Unintentional occurrences of this issue may arise due to:

▪️ uneven rolling of fat between dough layers
▪️ tears in the dough
▪️ cuts in the dough
▪️ premature melting of fat before baking, causing the dough layers to fuse

For easier rolling of fat without damaging dough layers, it is crucial to use pliable fat of similar density to the dough.

If opting for butter, it should be slightly cooled (~16–18 °C) and, optionally, mixed with a small amount of flour.

To prevent tearing, the use of strong flour is recommended, with adequate resting time for the dough between lamination sessions.

Sometimes the dough is intentionally pierced, or small cuts are made in a random fashion to avoid excessive separation. Additionally, pressing along the edges of puff pastry can prevent it from complete separation into individual thin layers during baking.

### Tip B1-40 — Blue Spots Between Honey Cake Layers and Filling
**Source lines:** 1160–1184

Body:

BLUE SPOTS BETWEEN HONEY CAKE LAYERS AND FILLING

On multiple occasions, concerned students have messaged me images of honey cakes exhibiting blue or bluish spots, accompanied by the question: "Marusya, is it safe to eat this? What's going on?"

❓ Can this cake be consumed?
✅ Absolutely

❓ What causes the appearance of blue spots?
❌ Blue spots indicate incomplete chemical reaction of baking soda in the cake layers

To be more specific, these blue spots don't develop within the cake layers themselves but rather at the junctions where the cake and its filling meet.

If baking soda fails to fully react, either during batter preparation or cake baking, the acid in berry fillings and/or sour cream frostings (lactic acid) reacts with the incompletely reacted baking soda in the cakes. Consequently, localized blue spots emerge.

❓ How to eliminate the risk of blue spots

Our primary objective is to ensure that most of the baking soda undergoes the reaction during the batter preparation, and only a small percentage is left for a swift and final reaction in the oven — higher temperatures speed up the process.

But to be on the safe side, after adding baking soda to the heated honey mixture, we can incorporate lemon juice or citric acid to monitor the reaction.

✅ If foaming occurs, albeit weakly, it indicates that the baking soda has already reacted adequately - > remove the mixture from the heat and add flour.

❌ If the mixture foams vigorously, signaling an incomplete reaction between baking soda and the acid in the honey, take corrective measures - > add a small amount of lemon juiceor further heat the mixture until the reaction weakens.

❗️Do not allow the mixture to overheat; otherwise, during the addition of flour to the batter, it may partially cook, resulting in a 'rubbery' texture. Similarly, when incorporating eggs into an overheated mixture, there is a risk of protein coagulation. To prevent this, use a water bath and/or actively stir with a whisk while introducing eggs.
