## Tip B4-03: Ganache — Sugar, Sorbitol (E420), and Aromatic Additives [Part 5]

**Body:**

Part 5

#marusya_about_ganache

In the previous part we discussed all the nuances of adding butter to a ganache. But apart from it, the following ingredients can be added to the ganache, namely:

➕ Sugar
🔺sugar in ganaches can be represented by: sucrose (=sugar), invert sugar or glucose syrup (high in dextrose)
💎 dose — at least 25% of the total weight

When added to a ganache, sugar influences its:

🔴 TASTE

▪️makes the ganache sweeter

🔴 TEXTURE

▪️makes it softer and more elastic
▪️increases the viscosity of the ganache, making it more stable due to its hygroscopic properties

🔴 SHELF LIFE

▪️ prolongs shelf life — sugar helps to "bind" water present in cream, making it unavailable for the microorganisms (molds) to multiply.

➕ Sorbitol or food supplement E420
It is an odorless white crystalline powder with a pleasant taste. It is two times less sweet than regular sugar and is very hygroscopic (absorbs and retains moisture).
💎 dose — 1.5–2.5% of the total weight

The way sorbitol affects the ganache is similar to sugar, however, E420 is used primarily as a stabilizer, and not as a sweetener.

➕ Aromatic additives
🔺 can be represented by various extracts, essential oils, alcohol, as well as flavored cream or milk
🔺 all additives that are sensitive to microflora (cocoa powder, spices, etc.) should be boiled for several minutes along with the cream

## Tip B4-05: Why Do You Need to Sift the Flour?

**Body:**

WHY DO YOU NEED TO SIFT THE FLOUR?

Almost every pastry recipe calls for sifted flour. Have you ever thought about why you should sift it?

Let's start with what we have actually moved away from for a long time, thanks to modern production technologies.

In the past, it was always necessary to sift the flour in order to:

▪️ get rid of weevils and other bugs
⚠️Nowadays contaminated foods should be disposed immediately.⚠️
▪️ get rid of the smallest pieces of stone for grinding grain — quern. The saddle quern consisted of a flat stone bed and a rounded stone to be operated manually against it; later, millstones developed from those hand-operated querns
▪️ get rid of debris — threads from packing bags, cereals from a neighboring box, pieces of paper or plastic bags, etc.

Nowadays, here are a few times you should sift your flour for baking:

▪️ to eliminate and prevent lumps — when your flour has been sitting around for a while, seems to be tightly packed, or has been stored at high humidity, lumps form even in high-quality flour.
▪️ to get a homogeneous, uniform base for the dough — sifted flour mixes better with other ingredients, and the dough behaves more predictably
▪️ to get much lighter and airier flour — it's especially important when baking sponges
▪️ to guarantee you get the right amount when the recipe uses cups instead of grams as a unit of measure (American recipes) — sifted flour will have a larger volume than not sifted

💡 Lifehack from Marusya

If the flour you are using is fairly fresh and does not contain any lumps, in most cases you can skip sifting, and make your flour airier simply by mixing it with a whisk.

## Tip B4-06: Honey Cake Layers — Factors That Affect the Intensity of Honey Taste and Aroma

**Body:**

HONEY CAKE LAYERS: FACTORS THAT AFFECT THE INTENSITY OF HONEY TASTE AND AROMA

Honey cake layers can have a different intensity of honey taste and aroma: from delicate, subtle honey notes to strong, pronounced ones. What does it depend on?

The very first and seemingly obvious answer that comes to mind is the amount of honey added. But in fact, this is far from the primary factor.

1️⃣ The intensity of the honey taste and aroma in the cake layers depends primarily on the heat treatment the batter undergoes before baking — the more you heat the honey mixture, the more pronounced taste will have the cake layers:

🔸subtle honey taste and aroma can be obtained by bringing the mixture for the honey dough to 60–70 °C over a water bath or in a saucepan
🔸 moderately pronounced taste is obtained by bringing the honey or the honey mixture to a boil (100–116 °C)
🔸 rich, pronounced honey taste and aroma of the cake layers is achieved by boiling the honey mixture or by using the dough made with burnt honey (> 116 °C)

2️⃣ Secondly, the intensity of the honey taste and aroma of the cake layers depends on the type of honey. If you want to get cakes with a light, delicate honey taste, opt for light types of honey, for example, acacia or linden. Buckwheat honey and other dark types should be preferred if you like cakes with a rich, pronounced honey taste.

3️⃣ The amount of honey added to the dough makes less difference to the intensity of honey taste and aroma of cake layers compared to the type of honey and its heat treatment. Therefore, do not be surprised that the amount of honey in honey recipes can vary from 60 to 450 g, depending on the recipe.

## Tip B4-07: Brown vs. White Eggs — Is There a Difference?

**Body:**

BROWN VS. WHITE EGGS — IS THERE A DIFFERENCE?

#marusya_theory_tips

Guys, you probably noticed that in some countries in supermarkets the prices for brown and white eggs differ? Is there any reason for that? Or is it just a marketing ploy?

Let's figure it out. But first things first.

Is there really a difference between brown and white eggs?

✔️ Yes — in color

Eggs come in many colors, depending on the breed of the hen. Chickens have different pigment genes that determine the color of the egg shell. Generally speaking, white shell eggs come from hens with white feathers and white earlobes, while brown shell eggs are produced by hens with brown feathers and red earlobes.
📍some chickens have a unique pigment gene and lay multi-colored eggs — light blue, green and even pink

✖️ No — in terms of nutritional value and taste

The color of the eggshell is not indicative of the best quality of the egg. In fact, there is no distinguishable difference between brown and white eggs when it comes to taste and nutritional value.
📍The nutritional value and palatability of an egg depend solely on the diet and environment of the chicken. The food eaten by the hen can directly affect how an egg tastes, which means that pasture-raised hens usually lay eggs that have a richer taste and higher concentration of useful macro- and microelements

❓ Is the higher price for brown eggs a marketing ploy?

✖️No — hens with brown feathers tend to be larger than hens with white feathers, meaning they consume more feed. Therefore, brown eggs need to be sold at a higher price to make up for the extra costs

✔️ Yes — if you have faced an unscrupulous dealer speculating on their more "natural" appearance

## Tip B4-11: How to Recalculate the Chocolate Percentage

**Body:**

HOW TO RECALCULATE THE CHOCOLATE PERCENTAGE🍫

#marusya_about_chocolate

More than enough times, I've been faced with complaints about broken ganache and tearful pleas for help.

I will help.

In 99% of cases, beginners simply replace the amount of chocolate specified in the recipe with exactly the same amount of chocolate with a different percentage of cocoa products.

DON'T DO THIS

Is it possible basically to replace one chocolate with another?

✔️ Yes, if you correctly calculate the required amount of chocolate with a cocoa butter content that is different from that specifed in the recipe

Let's say the recipe says 400 g of dark chocolate 54% (chocolate A). You only have 64% chocolate (Chocolate B) available.

How to recalculate?

The 54% marking reflects the total content of cocoa products (cocoa butter and cocoa liquor). For instance, 100 g of 54% chocolate may contain 45 g of sugar (45%), 19 g of cocoa liquor (19%) and 36 g of cocoa butter (36%).
📌 see the cocoa butter content on the package or clarify with the manufacturer

Enter the factor value

factor = cocoa butter content/100

The recipe uses 400 g of 54% chocolate (which is 19% cocoa liquor and 36% cocoa butter), and you want to replace it with 64% chocolate, which contains 41% cocoa butter

factor A (chocolate 54%) = 36% (cocoa butter)/100 = 0.36
factor B (chocolate 64%) = 41% (cocoa butter)/100 = 0.41

We have calculated the factor value for both types of chocolate, now we need to calculate how much of the available chocolate 64% we will need, according to the formula:

x = (weight of chocolate A * factor A) / factor B

Substitute the values:

x = (400 g * 0.36) / 0.41 = 351 g

That is, you can replace 400 g of 54% chocolate with 351 g of 64% chocolate that you have available.

## Tip B4-12: 8 Types of Butter and How to Use Them — Part 1 (Sweet Cream, Salted, Ghee, Compound)

**Body:**

8 TYPES OF BUTTER AND HOW TO USE THEM

#marusya_about_butter

Any butter is made from cream. But depending on the origin of the cream, the composition of the butter and ways of preparation, there are several varieties that ensure different tastes of meals and serve for different purposes:

🧈Sweet Cream Butter (Unsalted Butter)

It is the most popular variety due to its versatility: it can be used both for frying vegetables, meat, and for making desserts and pastries. It is made only from cream of animal origin.

🧈Salted Butter

Unsalted butter is all cream, while the salted variety has some salt added. Salt acts as a preservative, so this variety of butter lasts longer than the unsalted one. Most often, it is used for spreading on bread, topping veggies and pasta.

🧈Ghee butter

Gee is nothing more than the pure butterfat! It is cooked over low heat to remove the water molecules until the milk solids brown lightly, creating a slightly nutty, caramelized aroma. Ghee butter is shelf-stable and has a high smoke point. It is most commonly used for sautéing vegetables, meat, but it's also used in baked goods thanks to its rich flavor.

🧈 Compound butter

This is a special variety of butter that has been softened to a pliable state, and then has herbs, spices, and/or sweeteners (fruit or berry purees) added to it. Usually chilled and then sliced, compound butter can add herby richness to a steak, enhance the flavor of a baked good, or add creaminess to a pasta sauce.

Sea salt not only enhances the taste of foods, but can also add different flavors depending on the additives in its composition. It is much less salty than table or rock salt, so when cooking, you need much more of it to make the dish salty.

▫️Fleur de Sel ("flower of salt" in French)
The most expensive among sea salts. It has an exceptional taste and is able to give dishes a special delicacy and sophistication. This salt has snowflake-like crystals which add a crunchy texture to dishes.

▪️LAKE SALT

It is extracted from the bottom of salt lakes, where it settles naturally. Then it undergoes purification processes, after which the salt crystals are converted into food salt. Lake salt has a low sodium chloride content, so it is considered less salty.

Classification of salt on the basis of salinity levels from higher to lower:

1. Table salt "Extra" grade
2. Table salt of the first, second and top grades
3. Rock salt
4. Sea salt
5. Lake salt

In all my recipes I use rock salt. Therefore, if you use any other type of salt, make sure to reduce / increase its amount based on the degree of salinity.

## Tip B4-17: Why Add Salt to Desserts? — Functions 1-4 [Part 1]

**Body:**

WHY ADD SALT TO DESSERTS?
part 1

When I began to study this question, it immediately became clear that for this topic I'll need more than one post. You may wonder what salt has to do with dessert. Let's have a look.

Firstly, in some cases it's really impossible to make a dessert without salt. Secondly, without it, most of sweet pastries become insipid.

Let's figure it out.

FUNCTIONS OF SALT IN BAKING AND MAKING DESSERTS

1. Tightens the gluten structure

Salt has a binding or strengthening effect on gluten — this allows the dough to rise and expand during baking. It's indispensable, for example, when baking bread, pastries, croissants or sponges.

📍It's impossible to get a tall sponge without adding salt to the batter. Salted batter will turn out more voluminous and stronger than unsalted.

2. Enhances the taste

Salt brings out the characteristic taste of pastry it is used in. When used in moderate amounts, it does not make the product taste salty, but simply enhances its best qualities. It turns insipid two-dimensional tastes into rich, three-dimensional ones.

Such an effect is achieved thanks to the right combination of different tastes. For example, when salt is added, sweet flavors become less sweet and more complex, while sour flavors become less prominent.

3. Neutralize bitterness

Salt neutralizes bitterness, so any bitter foods (e.g. coffee, dark chocolate, grapefruit) become more appetizing and tasty if you add a little salt.

📍 Grapefruit remains slightly bitter both fresh and baked even after removing the albedo (the whitish, spongy inner part of the rind of a citrus fruit). But salting the pulp helps to get rid of this specific aftertaste.

4. Stabilizes the egg white foam

It's thanks to the addition of that tiny pinch of salt that the egg whites, when whipped, start to increase in volume and become lush. Adding salt to your meringue will ensure that all of the protein chains have safely bonded together.

A similar effect can be obtained by adding acid (e.g. lemon juice).

HOT METHOD SECRETS

💎 The infused liquid must be heated over medium or low heat so that the heating occurs gradually.
💎 In the infusion process, I advise you to use as few dishes as possible to prepare ingredients. For example, it is better to remove the zest immediately into cream. ## Tip B4-20: Why Add Salt to Desserts — Functions 5-8

**Body:**

5. Balances sweetness

Salt helps to balance the taste of products with a pronounced sweetness, such as caramel, chocolate or meringue. Such products are usually treacly, and it's almost impossible to add other flavor accents to them, even if you think they will work well in your pastry. Salt added in low concentrations balances the sweetness and adds depth to other flavors, so that our taste buds perceive them as moderately sweet, although the amount of sugar remains unchanged.

Salt changes the balance of liquid in foods. It makes juicy berries and fruits release juices, leaving the fibrous material behind. If you are making a fruit dessert and you need to get the maximum juice, just add a pinch of salt to your fruits.

Our body needs a certain amount of salt (NaCl) to function properly, so it's natural that we tend to like the taste of salt. This does not mean that we should eat tons of salt a day, but using it in reasonable amounts in cooking and making desserts will help us stay healthy and enjoy food.

## Tip B4-22: Everything You Need to Know About Agar — Part 1 (Overview, Quality, Gel Strength, vs. Gelatin)

**Body:**

EVERYTHING YOU NEED TO KNOW ABOUT AGAR
part 1

In previous posts, we have already talked with you about gelatin - #marusya_about_gelatin and pectin - #marusya_about_pectin. Today I suggest analyzing the next, no less interesting thickener - agar, aka agar-agar, aka seaweed food thickener.

Agar means "seaweed" in Malaysian. Its gelling properties are said to be accidentally discovered in Japan in the 17th century by Mino Tarōzaemon. He found out that the chilled seaweed soup had a jelly-like consistency the next day.

Agar is extracted from red and brown algae, which undergo several stages of rinsing, drying and grinding.

Nowadays, this natural thickener is widely used in confectionery art. It's used to make zephyr, marmalade, fruit pastes (pastila), sweets, jelly and mousse desserts.

Agar can be classified by its quality and gel strength.

🌿Quality

🔸 High grade agar - has a white or light yellow color, although it can have a slightly grayish tint
🔸 First grade agar - varies from yellow to dark yellow color

🌿Gel strength

🔸 600-1200 g/cm2

The higher the number it has, the thicker gel can be obtained. The greater the strength of agar is, the less amount of it is needed to gel the mixture.

📎 You can check out the table that shows how to convert between different amounts of agar-agar depending on the gel strength in the carousel.

Agar is often referred to as a natural substitute for gelatin. However, these two thickeners have a number of differences.

AGAR VS GELATIN

1️⃣ Unlike gelatin, agar does not impart any taste, smell, or color to desserts.
2️⃣ In terms of gelling properties, agar is 10 times superior to gelatin
3️⃣ Unlike gelatin, agar activates in an acidic environment. Gelatin is not a good choice when it comes to preparing, for example, desserts with pineapple or kiwi - they contain enzymes that destroy proteins (87-92% of the composition of gelatin). But agar-agar perfectly gels such mixtures.
4️⃣ Unlike gelatin, agar is thermoreversible - it can be heated and frozen as many times as you like.

## Tip B4-23: Agar — Rules of Working With Agar [Part 2]

**Body:**

part 2

Please support this useful post with your like ❤️ ー this is extremely important for us now!

For those interested in the answer to the question from the previous post about using agar to make the orange confit for the "Orange-Dark Chocolate" honey cake: I chose it because the filling made with gelatin began to leak out after defrosting.

This is due to the fact that ice crystals formed in the orange confit during freezing, which broke the texture of the jelly. As a result, the filling lost its original properties and began to leak out after defrosting.

Will gelatin-based filling always leak out after defrosting? No, it won't. Moreover, sometimes after defrosting, a filling made with agar or pectin may also leak out. What does it depend on? First of all, it depends on the amount of unbound water in the composition, but this should be discussed in another post.

Today we'll continue examining the agar. So, here are the RULES OF WORKING WITH AGAR:

1️⃣ Agar does not dissolve in cold water ー it should be brought to 95-100 °C to completely dissolve, at this temperature it forms a viscous and transparent solution, which when cooled to 35-40 °C, turns into a thick and pure gel.

2️⃣ Agar does not require pre-soaking in water or mixing with sugar. In order to introduce agar into the mixture, it is enough just to add it in there! The mixture can be hot, warm or cold ー it does not matter.

3️⃣ Agar is a thermoreversible thickener. You can heat and freeze fillings made with it as many times as you like ー it won't lose its gelling properties.

4️⃣ One of the main advantages of agar over gelatin is the ability to activate in an acidic environment. The advantage over pectin is that agar gels well even in the absence of sugar in the mixture.

5️⃣ The thicker the desired consistency of the gel you want to obtain is, the stronger agar should be used and / or the more agar you need.

## Tip B4-24: Agar — How to Calculate the Required Amount (Dosage by Acidity and Texture) [Part 3]

Today we'll talk about how to correctly calculate the required amount of agar and what it depends on.

If you are making a dessert with agar using a recipe from an eminent chef, in order to get a stable result, be sure to pay attention to:

🔸 amount of agar — agar is a very strong thickener (10 times stronger than gelatin), so even a slight deviation of 0.1-0.2 g can change the texture of the gel
🔸 strength of agar — if the recipe says "5 g of agar 1200", to get a dessert "like from a chef" you need to either use agar of the same strength or use the one that is available and convert between its strength.

2-4 g of agar per 150-180 ml of liquid

soft texture — 1.5 g / 500 ml
thick texture — 5 g / 500 ml

## Tip B4-25: Agar — Storage and Substitutes (Gelatin, Pectin, Corn Starch) [Part 4]

**Body:**

part 4

Today will be the final part of a series of articles about agar. I'll tell you about the terms and conditions of storage, as well as possible agar substitutes.

🌿 TERMS AND CONDITIONS OF STORAGE

🔸Agar

The shelf life of agar varies from 2 to 3 years since the date of packaging. It is indicated on the packaging and may change depending on the formula and production technology of agar by a specific manufacturer.

Agar should be stored in a cool, dry place, protected from sunlight, in airtight packaging.

🔸 Products with agar

1️⃣ Agar-based confectionery products are stored about 24 to 36 hours at a temperature between +4 to -8°C
2️⃣ Fruit mixtures — up to 7 days in the refrigerator
3️⃣ Jam and other preservation — up to 1 month in a cool, dry place, protected from sunlight

🌿 AGAR SUBSTITUTES

🔸 Gelatin
Gelatin has a lower density after cooling than agar, so it is suitable for making more delicate desserts. If you want to get a dense and smooth texture, it is better to use agar.

In general, these thickeners can be considered interchangeable, but it should be kept in mind that the gelling ability of agar is much higher than that of gelatin.

💎 Substitute: 1 tsp. agar = 2-3 tsp. gelatin

ℹ️ It's not suitable for vegetarians, as gelatin is made from the bones and tendons of animals

🔸 Pectin

Pectin is the most beneficial and equivalent substitute for agar, but there are exceptions. Both products have the vegetal nature and the only difference is that pectin contains carbohydrates, and its calorie content is higher than that of agar.

A prerequisite for the "activation" of pectin is the presence of sugar. Accordingly, substitute agar for pectin in the making of sugar-free desserts is impossible.

💎 Substitute: 1 tsp. agar = 2-3 tsp. pectin

🔸 Corn Starch

Agar-agar can be substituted for starch. However, starch makes the filling opaque, when dissolved, that significantly narrows the range of its possibilities.

💎 Substitute: 1 tsp. agar = 2 tsp. starch

## Tip B4-28: Brown Butter (Beurre Noisette) — 6 Tips for the Perfect Result

BROWN BUTTER OR BEURRE NOISETTE

#marusya_theory_tips

In the comments under yesterday's post, I noticed that for some people the technique of making brown butter was new and it raised questions and doubts.

Brown or browned butter (fr. beurre noisette) are all different names for the same product obtained by heating regular butter - it melts, separates into butterfat and milk solids, the latter sink to the bottom of the pan and turn a deep brown color.

The browning (caramelization) of butter is the result of the milk proteins and sugars going through the Maillard reaction upon heating to 130 °C.

The recipe for brown butter can be found in the previous post. But I'd like to give you some tips so that everything goes perfectly, and you don't get nervous.

6 TIPS FOR GETTING THE PERFECT BEURRE NOISETTE

1️⃣ Use a thick-bottomed saucepan to ensure even heating of the butter.

2️⃣ The saucepan should be of a light color - it's more difficult to notice the change in the color of the butter against a dark background, and it can easily burn.

3️⃣ To get the perfect brown butter, I advise you to heat no more than 200 g of butter at a time in a small saucepan (d = 14-16 cm). If you need a larger amount, use a saucepan d = 22-26 cm.

4️⃣ When the butter gets frothy, start to constantly stir it with a whisk so that the milk solids do not burn.

5️⃣ After bringing the butter to 128 °C, remove the saucepan from the heat, while continuing to stir - within 30-40 seconds the residual heat of the saucepan will bring the butter to the desired slightly brownish color (it will heat up to 130-138 °C).

6️⃣ Immediately after this, pour the butter into a separate container to stop the cooking process. Otherwise, the temperature will continue to rise, and as soon as it exceeds 140 °C, the butter will burn.

## Tip B4-33: Crème Anglaise From A to Z — Composition and Basic Rules [Part 1]

**Body:**

CRÈME ANGLAISE FROM A TO Z
As it turned out, not everyone can properly make the crème anglaise (French for "English cream") or custard. There's nothing complicated about its preparation, but at the same time, to obtain a stable result, you need to follow a number of rules, as well as understand the processes that occur during its preparation.

So in order to avoid difficulties in working with the custard, you'll need to know some tips and tricks which I'm going to share with you today.

Let's start with the concept of crème anglaise and custard.

Custard is a combination of water, fats and sugars which becomes possible thanks to an emulsifying agent.

Under normal conditions, fats and water don't mix, but with the presence of an emulsifier it becomes possible.

Crème anglaise is a type of custard in which the emulsifying agent is the lecithin present in egg yolk.

Crème anglaise can be used as a standalone product, as well as a sauce (French version) or a base for making mousses, ice creams and creams (English version).

✅ Composition of crème anglaise:

🔸 70% - liquid (milk / milk + cream 1:1 / milk + fruit puree 1:1)
🔸 15% - egg yolks
🔸 15% - sugar

To make crème anglaise, mix egg yolks with sugar and boil the milk and/or cream. Pour 1/3 of milk into the yolk-sugar mixture, then pour everything back into a saucepan with the remaining milk. Bring to 82°C and remove from the stove.

Basically, you can use this recipe and get down to cooking. However, there's a huge possibility that something will go wrong.

‼️ To properly make crème anglaise, you should follow a number of rules:

🔸 all ingredients must be fresh, as the enzymes contained in milk catalyze many biochemical processes
🔸 all tools used to cook the custard must be perfectly clean
🔸 use an appropriate tool at each stage of preparation: a whisk - for beating the yolks with sugar, a silicone spatula - for cooking the cream
🔸 stick to the right temperatures - bring the cream to 82-83 °C to pasteurize it and obtain the desired consistency

## Tip B4-34: Crème Anglaise — Why It Can Curdle at 82°C (Yolk and Sugar Chemistry) [Part 2]

**Body:**

part 2

I received an interesting question:

How can one cook crème anglaise to 82°C and not get an "omelette"?

To answer this question, let's analyze the composition and properties of some ingredients that are used to make the custard.

🌿 Yolk

Composition:

🔸 50-54% - water
🔸 30-33% - fats and lipids
🔸 16-17 - proteins (including lecithin)
🔸 1% - carbohydrates and minerals

It is thanks to lecithin that fats form an amalgam (emulsion) with a liquid. That is, an egg is, in fact, a natural emulsion.

‼️ Lecithin must not be heated above 70°C

🌿 Sugar

1️⃣ Sugar is very hygroscopic, that is, it has a high ability to absorb moisture.

I suggest doing a little experiment. Place one yolk in a bowl, add a couple of tbsp of sugar and leave it like that without stirring.

After a while, you'll find that the mixture turned grainy - the sugar absorbed all the liquid from the yolk.

Therefore, when you start making crème anglaise, as soon as you add sugar to the yolks, IMMEDIATELY start to stir the mixture vigorously with a whisk! If during this process you decide to set it aside and answer a phone call, for example, the custard won't be of the right texture anymore - the emulsion will break, which is IMPOSSIBLE TO FIX! If you continue to cook the custard using this mixture, you'll get a lumpy mixture in your saucepan.

Next, we face another interesting task:

❌ As you remember, lecithin, which is found in the yolks, must not be heated above 70°C.

✅ But crème anglaise must be brought to 82-83°C in order to achieve the desired consistency and, most importantly, to pasteurize the product.

Here the 2nd property of sugar comes to the rescue:

2️⃣ Sugar prevents lecithin and proteins from overheating.

By adding sugar to the yolks, we thereby delay the destruction of lecithin, which allows us to bring the crème anglaise to 82°C and not cook it into an "omelette".

📌 Even if you thoroughly stirred the yolks with sugar, there still is a risk of curdling (coagulation) of proteins.

## Tip B4-35: Crème Anglaise — Correct Temperature Measurement Technique [Part 3]

**Body:**

part 3

As promised, today I'll tell you why, when making crème anglaise, you still might cook it into an "omelette" after bringing it to 82 °C, even if you thoroughly whisked the sugar with yolks, protecting the lecithin from overheating.

The reason for this is quite obvious - the custard was brought to a higher temperature.

To measure the temperature correctly, follow guidelines:

🔸 When cooking the custard, stir it with a spatula and not with a whisk to avoid air getting into the custard. When dealing with the aerated mixture it is almost impossible to measure the temperature correctly!

📌 Cooking crème anglaise with a whisk is considered a gross mistake!
❌ Firstly, you compromise the very concept of the custard by letting the air penetrate into it - thus you'll get a mousse, not a custard.
❌ Secondly, you won't be able to accurately measure the temperature of the mousse.

🔸 Stir the custard carefully, making figure-eight motion with spatula - this will ensure uniform heating of the entire mixture and prevent it from scorching.

🔸 Do not use a pyrometer to measure the temperature of the custard - it is designed to read the temperature on the surface! Use a probe thermometer istead, immersing it into the mixture (avoid touching the bottom of the pan!).

🔸 However, if you are cooking on an induction stove, I do not recommend using a probe thermometer to check the temperature of the custard - no matter how hard you try, the device won't read the temperature correctly, because:

❌ the bottom of the pans for induction stoves is metal, the probe is also metal, that is the induction heats it along with the pan - to put it another way, the probe thermometer will read not the temperature of the mixture, but the temperature the metal is heated to.

✅ Solution: If you have an induction stove, use a glass deep fry/candy thermometer

## Tip B4-36: Crème Anglaise — Complete Cooking Instructions (Step by Step) [Part 4]

**Body:**

Part 4

Complete cooking instructions

1️⃣ Whisk the egg yolks and sugar.

📌 Whisk thoroughly and quickly to avoid lumps. You need to ensure a good emulsion so that the yolks don't separate.

2️⃣ Heat the milk.

If you're using pasteurized or UHT milk, bring it to 85°C.

If you're using raw milk, bring it to a boil (100°C) to pasteurize.

📌 When the milk has boiled, a film forms on its surface - these are proteins that have coagulated. To prevent skin formation, add some sugar to the milk.
📌 It is best to use pasteurized dairy products to achieve a balanced taste in the final product.

3️⃣ Temper the egg yolks - radually pour a third of the hot milk into the yolk mixture in a thin stream and mix well with a silicone spatula.

❌ If you pour the milk too quickly, the yolks can cook, while you only need to heat up the yolk mixture.

4️⃣ Pour in the remaining milk, put the saucepan on low heat and pasteurize the mixture at 82-83°C, stirring continuously with a spatula.

❌ Do not cook it on high heat - the custard may burn!

5️⃣ Remove the custard from the heat and pour it into a wide plastic vessel.

📌 If you used flavors (vanilla pod / zest / herbs), strain the custard first.

6️⃣ Place the vessel in a shock freezer or over an ice bath for quick cooling.

It is vital to quickly cool the custard to room temperature, as it is made from milk and eggs, which spoil quickly.

7️⃣ Cooled custard:

a) if you're going to use crème anglaise as a standalone product or as a sauce, cover it with cling film in contact and place it in the fridge for further stabilization

b) if you're going to use it as a base for frosting - add it to the remaining products (e.g. melted chocolate), cover with cling film in contact and place in the fridge

c) if you're going to use it as a base for crémeux - first add melted gelatin mass, then quickly cool it, cover with cling film and place in the fridge

## Tip B4-37: 5 Problems When Making Crème Anglaise and How to Solve Them [Part 5, final]

**Body:**

5 PROBLEMS WHEN MAKING CRÈME ANGLAISE AND HOW TO SOLVE THEM

This is a final post about crème anglaise, in which I'll tell you what problems you may face in the process of making custard, and how to solve them.

1️⃣ Runny custard

❌ Reason
The custard hasn't been brought to 82-83 °C.

Crème anglaise contains the alpha-amylase enzyme which can break down protein.

If the custard has been brought to less than 74 °C, the alpha-amylase won't get destroyed and will digest the proteins. In other words, the custard will be runny. Such custard can't be fixed.

✅ Solution
If the custard has been brought to a temperature above 75 °C, the product has been pasteurized (alpha-amylase is destroyed), it can be fixed - just add bloomed gelatin in order to make the custard more stable.

2️⃣ Custard has curdled

❌ Reason
The custard was brought to a temperature above 82-83 °C.

✅ Solution
In the future, be sure to measure the temperature correctly following the rules described in the previous post (P3). You can find them by the hashtag #marusya_about_crèmeanglaise

Someone advises blending curdled custard. Thus, you will eliminate the "lump effect", but won't get rid of the omelette taste. Therefore, if your crème anglaise has split, I advise you to remake it so as not to compromise the whole cake.

3️⃣ Custard foams during cooking

❌ Reason
See what's in your hand. I'm sure it's a whisk.

✅ Solution
After beating the yolks with sugar, put the whisk aside and switch to a silicone spatula. This is the appropriate tool to cook the custard with.

4️⃣ There are yolk pieces in custard

❌ Reason
Poorly beaten yolks with sugar

✅ Solution
Remake the custard

I don't advise you to strain it. Yes, of course, you'll get rid of pieces of yolk floating in the custard, but this won't help you to get rid of the yolk taste.

5️⃣ Skin forms on the custard surface

❌ Reason
The custard wasn't covered with cling film or wasn't covered tightly enough.

✅ Solution
Strain the custard or carefully remove the skin. Then cover the crème anglaise with cling film touching the surface.

## Tip B4-40: How to Choose the Right Chocolate Fluidity

**Body:**

HOW TO CHOOSE THE RIGHT CHOCOLATE FLUIDITY

#marusya_theory_tips

Fluidity depends on the content of cocoa butter in chocolate - the more cocoa butter in its composition, the more fluid it is.

Chocolate with a higher fluidity contains more cocoa butter and when tempered properly, it allows you to create a hard, thin and crunchy chocolate coating with a glossy sheen.

Such chocolate contracts better during cooling, which makes it easier to remove the chocolate shell from the mold.

🌿 Chocolate with a lower fluidity contains less cocoa butter, which produces a less hard, less shiny and thicker chocolate coating.

Determining the fluidity of chocolate is not difficult, as manufacturers indicate it on the packaging with a drop symbol system. The more drops are drawn on the packaging that you hold in your hands, the more fluid the chocolate is in front of you.

There is a wide range of fluidity levels marked with 1-5 drops:

🔸 1 drop
✅ ideal for mousse and frosting

🔸 2 drops
✅ perfect for making large molded chocolate figures

🔸 3 drops
✅ versatile chocolate suitable for making any texture: frosting, ganache for coating, icing, chocolates

🔸 4-5 drops
✅ ideal for molded chocolates with a thin chocolate shell

That is, depending on the application, you should choose a chocolate with the right fluidity.

In my work, I most often use milk or dark chocolate with a fluidity of 3 drops - its properties are most suitable for making ganaches for coating, crémeux, as well as mousse and baked chocolate cheesecakes.

SEVERAL THICKENERS IN A FILLING. WHY?

In online courses and during live master classes, which I miss so much, students often ask me why they need to add gelatin if they have already added pectin to the preparation.

The question is really good and very interesting. Let's find the answer now!

The thing is that each thickener has different properties:

🌿 Agar-agar
🔸 gives a delicate, "glassy", not elastic texture
🔸 activates at high temperatures (90-100°C). If added to a cold mixture, it starts to set at 40°C and form lumps
🔸 must not be frozen as it may lose its properties
🔸 if you process the gelled mixture with a blender, you'll get a gel

🌿Gelatin
🔸 gives good elasticity and makes the fillings a bit "rubbery"
🔸 activates at low temperatures (can be added at 25-30°C)
🔸 retains its properties when frozen
🔸 it takes at least 6-7 hours for the filling to set
🔸 contains a large amount of protein, which allows one to whip it and get a thick foam

🌿 Pectin (NH, NH Nappage)
🔸gives a stable, jam-like texture and doesn't affect the taste of the mixture
🔸 activates at high temperatures (80-100°C)
🔸 retains its properties when frozen
🔸 fillings made with pectin can be reheated

Sometimes when we want our filling to get the properties of 2 different thickeners at once, we add gelatin or agar-agar to the pectin.

For example, when making the Kiwi-Lime-Bergamot cheesecake, the photo of which you can see in the carousel, I used both pectin (to give light elasticity and natural taste) and agar-agar (for stability) to prepare the Mojito marmalade made from vermouth, lime juice and bergamot.

In this case, the gelatin can't be used to stabilize (gel) the mixture. Can you guess why?
