### Tip B5-01 — Crème Anglaise: Finishing Steps (Tempering, Cooking, Cooling, Storage by Use)

**Body:**

3️⃣ Temper the egg yolks - radually pour a third of the hot milk into the yolk mixture in a thin stream and mix well with a silicone spatula.

### Tip B5-02 — 5 Problems When Making Crème Anglaise and How to Solve Them

**Body:**

This is a final post about crème anglaise, in which I'll tell you what problems you may face in the process of making custard, and how to solve them.

1️⃣ Runny custard

❌ Reason
The custard hasn't been brought to 82-83 °C.

Crème anglaise contains the alpha-amylase enzyme which can break down protein.

If the custard has been brought to less than 74 °C, the alpha-amylase won't get destroyed and will digest the proteins. In other words, the custard will be runny. Such custard can't be fixed.

✅ Solution
If the custard has been brought to a temperature above 75 °C, the product has been pasteurized (alpha-amylase is destroyed), it can be fixed - just add bloomed gelatin in order to make the custard more stable.

2️⃣ Custard has curdled

❌ Reason
The custard was brought to a temperature above 82-83 °C.

✅ Solution
In the future, be sure to measure the temperature correctly following the rules described in the previous post (P3). You can find them by the hashtag #marusya_about_crèmeanglaise

Someone advises blending curdled custard. Thus, you will eliminate the "lump effect", but won't get rid of the omelette taste. Therefore, if your crème anglaise has split, I advise you to remake it so as not to compromise the whole cake.

3️⃣ Custard foams during cooking

❌ Reason
See what's in your hand. I'm sure it's a whisk.

✅ Solution
After beating the yolks with sugar, put the whisk aside and switch to a silicone spatula. This is the appropriate tool to cook the custard with.

4️⃣ There are yolk pieces in custard

❌ Reason
Poorly beaten yolks with sugar

✅ Solution
Remake the custard

I don't advise you to strain it. Yes, of course, you'll get rid of pieces of yolk floating in the custard, but this won't help you to get rid of the yolk taste.

5️⃣ Skin forms on the custard surface

❌ Reason
The custard wasn't covered with cling film or wasn't covered tightly enough.

✅ Solution
Strain the custard or carefully remove the skin. Then cover the crème anglaise with cling film touching the surface.

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

My students often ask me which cream is better to use.

First of all, I'd like to say that in my recipes I only use cream of animal origin. I don't work with plant-based creams for a number of reasons:

🔻 they contain a huge amount of stabilizers, flavors, preservatives and other synthetic components

🔻 contain palm or palm kernel oil. During the preparation of vegetable cream, this oil is partially hydrogenated, that is, the liquid emulsion turns into solid fatty acids.

❌ raise of cholesterol levels, negative effect on the immune and cardiovascular systems

✅ The number one goal that I pursue when developing new recipes is to get the perfectly balanced taste. And these are the FAT MOLECULES that contribute to the FLAVOR! Therefore, I prefer to use dairy products with a high fat content.

### Tip B5-05 — How to Choose the Right Chocolate Fluidity

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

Fluidity depends on the content of cocoa butter in chocolate - the more cocoa butter in its composition, the more fluid it is.

Chocolate with a higher fluidity contains more cocoa butter and when tempered properly, it allows you to create a hard, thin and crunchy chocolate coating with a glossy sheen.

Such chocolate contracts better during cooling, which makes it easier to remove the chocolate shell from the mold.

🌿 Chocolate with a lower fluidity contains less cocoa butter, which produces a less hard, less shiny and thicker chocolate coating.

Determining the fluidity of chocolate is not difficult, as manufacturers indicate it on the packaging with a drop symbol system. The more drops are drawn on the packaging that you hold in your hands, the more fluid the chocolate is in front of you.

There is a wide range of fluidity levels marked with 1-5 drops:

🔸 1 drop
✅ ideal for mousse and frosting

🔸 2 drops
✅ perfect for making large molded chocolate figures

🔸 3 drops
✅ versatile chocolate suitable for making any texture: frosting, ganache for coating, icing, chocolates

🔸 4-5 drops
✅ ideal for molded chocolates with a thin chocolate shell

That is, depending on the application, you should choose a chocolate with the right fluidity.

In my work, I most often use milk or dark chocolate with a fluidity of 3 drops - its properties are most suitable for making ganaches for coating, crémeux, as well as mousse and baked chocolate cheesecakes.

### Tip B5-06 — Several Thickeners in a Filling. Why?

**Body:**

In online courses and during live master classes, which I miss so much, students often ask me why they need to add gelatin if they have already added pectin to the preparation.

The question is really good and very interesting. Let's find the answer now!

The thing is that each thickener has different properties:

🌿 Agar-agar
🔸 gives a delicate, "glassy", not elastic texture
🔸 activates at high temperatures (90-100°C). If added to a cold mixture, it starts to set at 40°C and form lumps
🔸 must not be frozen as it may lose its properties
🔸 if you process the gelled mixture with a blender, you'll get a gel

🌿Gelatin
🔸 gives good elasticity and makes the fillings a bit "rubbery"
🔸 activates at low temperatures (can be added at 25-30°C)
🔸 retains its properties when frozen
🔸 it takes at least 6-7 hours for the filling to set
🔸 contains a large amount of protein, which allows one to whip it and get a thick foam

🌿 Pectin (NH, NH Nappage)
🔸gives a stable, jam-like texture and doesn't affect the taste of the mixture
🔸 activates at high temperatures (80-100°C)
🔸 retains its properties when frozen
🔸 fillings made with pectin can be reheated

Sometimes when we want our filling to get the properties of 2 different thickeners at once, we add gelatin or agar-agar to the pectin.

For example, when making the Kiwi-Lime-Bergamot cheesecake, the photo of which you can see in the carousel, I used both pectin (to give light elasticity and natural taste) and agar-agar (for stability) to prepare the Mojito marmalade made from vermouth, lime juice and bergamot.

In this case, the gelatin can't be used to stabilize (gel) the mixture. Can you guess why?

### Tip B5-07 — What You Need to Know About Pectin, Part 1 (What Is Pectin)

**Body:**

part 1

We've already talked with you about gelatin, and today I'd like to start a new series of posts about the super-popular thickener - pectin.

So, let's start with finding out what pectin is.

Pectin (from the Greek "pektos" - 'firm and hard') is a polysaccharide (high-molecular weight carbohydrate) that is present in foods and has an important function, which is to ensure their quality and texture.

In the culinary arts, pectin acts as a gelling agent, stabilizer, thickener, and water-retaining agent.

Like some other thickeners (cornstarch, agar-agar, gelatin), pectin in small quantities thickens the mixture, and in large quantities - gellies it.

It is used to make jams, confits, jellies, marmalades, fruit pastes (pastila) and much more.

The main advantage of pectin compared to other thickeners is that it is able to form a sufficiently thick texture WITHOUT AFFECTING THE TASTE, SMELL AND COLOR OF THE FINISHED PRODUCT.

Pectin is obtained by aqueous extraction of the appropriate edible plant materials, mainly from citrus peel and apple pomace, sugar-beet pulp or sunflower heads, unlike, for example, gelatin, which is obtained by boiling skin, tendons, ligaments, and/or bones with water.

Interestingly, some fruits (apple, plum, currant) contain so much pectin that one can make jelly or jam from them without adding pectin.

📌I added a table which shows the content of pectin in fruits and berries to the carousel.

🌱The strength of pectin

To standardize pectins, the USA-SAG method is used: a gel is prepared from the pectin under study, sugar, water and acid. While hot, this pectin solution is bottled in standard cups and set aside until set. The single gels are removed carefully from the cups and transferred on a plate which allows one to measure the percentage of sagging of the gel cone under its own weight with the help of a ridgelimeter. From this value the °SAG can be calculated.

The majority of pectins are standardized to 150 grade USA-SAG (the strength of almost any pectin you find in pastry stores).

### Tip B5-08 — What You Need to Know About Pectin, Part 2 (Powdered vs Liquid; Chemical Properties)

**Body:**

part 2

Thanks to your comments I decided not to put writing the continuation of the post on pectins on the back-burner. Today, as promised, I will tell you about the types of pectin.

There is no unified world classification of pectins. Each manufacturer creates its own unique product! However, pectins can be classified according to a number of properties.

So, here are the classifications of pectin:

🌱 According to the form it comes in

📌 POWDERED

Should be mixed with sugar and gradually sprinkled into the mixture at a temperature of 40-45°C.

📌 LIQUID (EXTRACT)

Should be diluted in hot water and only then added to the mixture. This type of pectin is less common as it can only be used if the mixture can hold the extra liquid. It is more suitable for large productions.

🌱 By chemical properties

During the production, pectin undergoes an esterification process — it interacts with alcohol and acid, which gives it various properties.

📌 Low ester (the degree of esterification (DE) is less than 50%) — is able to gel regardless of the acidity of fruits/berries or amount of sugar, but in the presence of calcium ions.

This type of pectin works with dairy-based and low sugar mixtures.

📌 High ester (DE is 50% or more) — is able to gel at high acidity (low pH) and high sugar content of the mixture. This is the most popular type of pectin — it dissolves faster in water and gels mixtures faster.

In the pastry business, high ester pectin is used to make zephyr, fruit pastes (pastila) and marmalade.

📌 Amidated pectin is a low ester pectin which undergoes ammonia treatment during the manufacturing process. Gelation requires less calcium in neutral pH mixtures and is less prone to setting (gelling) at high calcium concentrations.

Amidated pectin is often used to make fruit fillings for yogurts and thermostable fillings.

### Tip B5-09 — What You Need to Know About Pectin, Part 3 (Thermal Reversibility; Setting Speed; Apple/Citrus Pectin)

**Body:**

part 3

So, guys, today is Sunday, and it means that we'll have a theory today. When our brain, body and soul are relaxed, they are best able to absorb information.

Today we continue to talk about the classifications of pectin. The pectin can also be classified according to:

🌱 Thermal reversibility:

📌Thermoreversible - such pectin becomes liquid when heated and then solidifies when cooled, that is, the mixture can be reheated and cooled again until set.

📌 Regular - once it gels, it cannot be reheated because pectin loses its properties at temperatures above 90-100 °C.

🌱Time of setting (gelling)

📌 Rapid set pectin
🔸 degree of esterification - above 70-76%
🔸 gelling temperature - 75-85°С
🔸 gelling time — 10-15 minutes

📌 Medium rapid set pectin
🔸 degree of esterification - 70-72%
🔸 gelling temperature - 60-70°С
🔸 gelling time — 15-20 minutes

📌 Slow set pectin
🔸 degree of esterification - 56-68%
🔸 gelling temperature - 45-60°С
🔸 gelling time - 20-25 minutes

If we try to analyze these numbers, we can notice a consistent pattern:

✅ The higher the degree of esterification is, the faster the gelling happens, and the higher the temperature is needed to form gel.

The speed of gelation is regulated by chemical compounds in the composition of pectin.

Each manufacturer "plays" with pectin compositions and forms its own assortment. On the one hand, this allows you to choose pectin for absolutely any confectionery purposes, on the other hand, it complicates the issue of their interchangeability.

🌱 Apple or citrus pectin

This is basic, regular pectin. It is obtained from the peels, seeds and pulp of citrus fruits. Has nothing but pectin in its composition. This pectin is classified as high ester, that is, to form a stable gel, this type of pectin needs a large amount of sugar in the composition, the presence of acid and high temperature.

🌱 Pectin NH

🌱 Nappage

🌱 Pectin NH Plus

Medium ester pectin, which does not form a dense marmalade texture, but thickens well, producing an elastic texture. With this pectin mixtures set faster than with NH pectin. It is used to prepare all kinds of fillings and glazes.

### Tip B5-11 — What You Need to Know About Pectin, Part 5 (Pectin FX, Acid-Free, Slow Set)

**Body:**

part 5

In the previous posts we've already discussed such main types of pectin as regular pectin (apple / citrus / yellow), pectin NH, NH Plus and pectin Nappage. Today I suggest analyzing the remaining three types:

🌱 Pectin FX

Low ester pectin, which only works with calcium-containing foods; the level of acidity does not matter. Most often it is used to make milk and cream fillings, marmalade or confiture.

📍It also works with dairy products regardless of the amount of sugar in the mixture, which allows you to get a non-sweet, balanced taste of creamy fillings.

🌱 Acid-free pectin

In fact, this is an analogue of regular pectin, for which the level of acidity does not matter since it contains calcium sulfate which has good hygroscopic, stabilizing and emulsifying properties.

It is used when working with fruits and berries that do not contain acid, e.g. bananas, apples, coconuts, dates, watermelon, etc. It also works well with nut butters. For instance, the pistachio paste marmalade is made using acid-free pectin.

🌱 Slow set pectin

Another analogue of regular pectin. The key difference between them is that slow set pectin gels mixtures much more slowly. This is very convenient if, for example, you need to pour a filling or marmalade into small molds - it gives you 3-4 minutes for this, while any other pectin sets almost instantly.

Knowing the properties of each type of pectin, you can easily choose the right one for any of your confectionery experiments and thus, obtain the best result.

### Tip B5-12 — What You Need to Know About Pectin, Part 6 (Methods of Adding Pectin to a Mixture)

**Body:**

part 6

Today we'll talk about ways of adding pectin to the mixture being gelled and will choose the best of them.

1️⃣Pre-dissolve pectin in boiling water

Add pectin powder to boiling water, carefully processing it with a hand blender. Then pour it in a thin stream into the hot mixture being gelled, stirring it with a whisk.

This method is used if the mixture can "hold" additional liquid. It's more suitable for large productions.

📍Proportions:

Pectin - 5-8%
Water - 92-95%

2️⃣ Pre-dissolve pectin in sugar syrup

Add pectin to the sugar syrup and stir thoroughly until the pectin has completely dissolved. Then add it to the mixture being gelled.

3️⃣ Mix pectin and sugar

This is the most common and convenient way. Vigorously stirring the warm mixture (35-50 °C) with a whisk, gradually sprinkle in the sugar, pre-mixed with pectin.

📍Bear in mind that at temperatures below 35 °C, pectin may start to set, form lumps, and thus, it will be difficult to stir the mixture and get a good texture.

❓ Why is it necessary to mix sugar and pectin, and not add them separately?

The whole trick is the interaction of pectin with water.

A particle of pectin powder, once in water, absorbs it like a sponge, increasing in size several times, and only after reaching a certain size starts to dissolve.

If pectin powder particles, when in contact with water, are close to each other, then, in the process of absorbing water and swelling, they stick together, forming a sticky lump that slowly dissolves in water. To avoid this, pectin should be pre-mixed with sugar in a ratio of 1:10 or more.

### Tip B5-13 — What You Need to Know About Pectin, Part 7 (How Much Pectin to Use)

part 7

You can know everything about pectins, but when it comes to creating some kind of filling or dessert using it, the question arises: how to decide how much pectin to use?

Among the huge number of questions about pectin, this is perhaps one of the most important.

Let's find the answer😉

To start the gelling process, you need 3 components:

1️⃣ sugar
2️⃣ pectin
3️⃣ acid or calcium

Scientists consider the ratio 60 : 1 : 1 to be optimal

🔸 60 parts of sugar
🔸 1 part of pectin
🔸 1 part of acid (citric, tartaric, lactic, etc.)

However, this ratio may vary depending on:

🔺 the type of pectin you are using (yellow, NH, FX);
🔺 pectin formula, which each manufacturer keeps in strict confidence;
🔺 the initial content of pectin in berries or fruits that you are going to use;

📍 the more pectin the fruit you work with contains, the less pectin you need to introduce additionally

### Tip B5-14 — Things We Need to Know About Eggs, Part 1 (Egg Anatomy: Shell, Cuticle, Membranes)

**Body:**

part 1

#marusya_likbez

I haven't posted theory for a while, so let's revive this tradition again.

I'm planning to post some useful information on thickeners - pectin, gelatin, agar-agar, starch and much more. I feel that I need to speak out to eliminate your doubts concerning their interchangeability.

Feel free to suggest your topic for the next useful post in the comments. And meanwhile, let's talk about ... EGGS.

We all know that an egg consists of a shell, and inside there is a yolk and white. But apart from them, there are many more other components inside the egg.

In fact, the anatomy of an egg is the following:
🥚 shell (+ cuticle)
🥚 outer and inner membrane
🥚 air chamber (air cell)
🥚 chalaza
🥚 vitelline membrane
🥚 yolk
🥚 egg white

SHELL is the first line of defense against air and bacteria. The shell weight makes up approximately 10% of the total egg weight.

Eggshell contains 7 to 17 thousand tiny pores. These pores are responsible for the movement of air and the penetration of bacteria into the egg, and let the carbon dioxide and moisture escape the egg.

You may ask where the extra water and carbon dioxide come from in the egg. We will definitely talk about this when it comes to egg aging and shelf life

CUTICLE is a natural coating that covers the surface of the egg and helps to fill the pores in order to protect the egg against air and bacteria.

❗️ Egg washing reduces this protection, leading to a more quick egg spoilage. The likelihood of bacteria getting into the egg increases.

OUTER AND INNER MEMBRANE - these are the layers that separate the shell from the egg white.

As the egg ages, the protective shell dries up and begins to flake off, allowing the outside air to enter the egg shell through the small pores and be stored in the AIR CHAMBER for chick to breathe

Why are fresh eggs so hard to peel? Until a few weeks have passed, the amount of air accumulated between the membranes won't allow them to easily detach and peel the shell from the hard-boiled egg white.

❗️Therefore, by putting a hard-boiled egg into cold water, we do not make it easier to peel it, but significantly reduce its shelf life.

### Tip B5-16 — Things We Need to Know About Eggs, Part 3 (Egg White / Albumin Structure and Proteins)

**Body:**

part 3

#marusya_likbez

Finally, my hands reached the point of writing to you about the most intriguing component of the egg for us, confectioners — egg white.

Egg white (albumin) is actually consist of four parts: an internal thick white, an internal thin white, an external thick white and an external thin white. These layers are designed to protect and cushion the chick embryo as it develops.

The white of a freshly laid egg will be turbid, not quite transparent. This is because it contains carbon dioxide, which slowly escapes through the shell as the egg ages, turning the white turbid color to transparent.

All layers of the egg white become more watery as the egg ages. Therefore, the old egg will spread in a thin layer on the pan, while the fresh one will be fried in quite thick layer. Noticed?

In general, during storage of eggs, all nutrients gradually decompose into water and carbon dioxide, which eventually come out through the pores in the shell.

❗️This is why old eggs always weigh less than fresh ones.

Egg white contains proteins, as well as niacin, riboflavin, magnesium, potassium and sodium. It is not as nutritious as the yolk, but it has only about 17 calories and does not contain fat and cholesterol.

Proteins found in eggs:

✅Ovotransferrin and ovalbumin

🔸 Responsible for coagulation and heat resistance of the egg, so the older the egg, the more time it takes to cook it. If you use too old eggs, the meringue may not bake properly at all.

Now egg white powder — albumin becomes more and more popular. There are 3 types of albumin, but only one of them is ideal for confectionery purposes.

### Tip B5-18 — Things We Need to Know About Eggs, Part 5 (Egg Disinfection: Production vs Home)

Today we will talk about the disinfection of eggs. This topic is important and interesting for all pastry professionals: both home bakers and those who work in production.

First of all, it is worth noting that the disinfection of eggs at home and in production is very different.

✅ Egg disinfection in production

In production, eggs are disinfected in special rooms. First, the eggs are sorted, randomly ovoscoped (using a special device with a beam of light passing through the egg so that one is able to see what happens inside), and then transferred to 4-section baths for disinfection. In each section, the egg goes through a certain stage:

🔸 1st section - the eggs are placed in warm water for 5-10 minutes
🔸 2d section - disinfection with a 0.5% sodium carbonate solution (at 40-45 °C) for 5-10 minutes
🔸 3d section - disinfection with a 2% bleach solution or 0.5% chloramine solution for 5 minutes
🔸 4th section - the eggs are rinsed with running water for 5 minutes.

✅ Egg disinfection at home

There are a large number of disinfectants that are suitable for disinfecting eggs at home. For example, Nettex, Battles, Defra and others.

Regular washing with baking soda will not fully protect against the risk of salmonella infection. Moreover, such a procedure will destroy the protective shell of the egg, and the risk of pathogens entering will increase significantly.

In addition, when cooking curds, custards and egg white creams made from raw eggs, the temperature is not high enough to sterilize the eggs. Therefore, there is always a small, but still possible risk of getting infected with salmonella.

However, if you are very meticulous in this matter, there are 3 ways:

1. Do not cook homemade custards and curds made from raw eggs.
2. Always use disinfectants.
3. Switch to pasteurized eggs or dried powder (dried egg white or egg powder).

### Tip B5-19 — Things We Need to Know About Eggs, Part 6 (Substituting Fresh Eggs with Dried Egg Powder — Formula)

**Body:**

part 6

#marusya_likbez

Today I'd like to explain to you how to work with dried egg powders.

Most often they are used in mass production, but some home bakers also tend to use powders in order to zero out the risk of salmonella infection when preparing frostings made from raw eggs.

In order to understand how to substitute a regular fresh egg for dried egg powder, you need to figure out how much dry matter the egg itself contains.

Standard dry matter content:

🔸 Whole egg (egg melange) - 27% dry matter
🔸 Egg yolk - 46% dry matter
🔸 Egg white - 12% dry matter

There is a formula by which you can calculate the required amount of dried egg powder (G₂):

G₁*a₁
G₂ = __________
a₂

G₁ is the mass of the product being substituted - for example, you need to substitute 2000 g of fresh eggs for dried egg powder

a₁ is the dry matter content of the product being substituted - as you now know, the whole egg contains 27% dry matter

a₂ - dry matter of the substitute product, that is, the powder itself - as a rule, the percentage of dry matter content is indicated on the packaging, ~ 94%

Thus, you get:

2000 g * 27%
G₂ = _______________ = 574 g
94%

That is, in order to substitute 2 kg of fresh eggs, you need 574 g of dried egg powder.

Lastly, it's necessary to find out how much water is needed to dilute the powder:

G₁—G₂

2000 g - 574 g = 1426 g

Easy as ABC 😉

If you need to substitute only the egg white or egg yolk, then use the percentage of dry matter in each of them (46% and 12%, correspondingly) in the formula and get the required amount of dried egg white or egg yolk.

### Tip B5-22 — About Food Colorings, Part 3 (Water-Soluble vs Fat-Soluble)

**Body:**

PART 3

#marusya_foodcolorings

Speaking about colorings we've come to a very important classification into water- and fat-soluble: I will tell you how they differ and how to choose the right type.

🌿 Water-soluble

This type of colorings is used to color foods with a high water content.

🔺liquid water-soluble clorings are suitable for water-based glazes, marshmallows, zephyr or fruit pastes (pastila);

🔺 gel water-soluble colorings, in which the percentage of moisture is lower than that of liquid ones, can be used to color sponges, low-fat frostings, as well as to create all kinds of patterns on confectionery products by applying the colorings to a pastry bag;

🔺powder (dry) water-soluble colorings are suitable for coloring products that are highly sensitive to moisture: for example, meringues or macarons.

🌿 Fat-soluble

Fat-soluble colorings do an excellent job of coloring high-fat mixtures without forming lumps and curdling, giving the desired color to products.

Are a good choice for coloring chocolate used for decoration, creating velour mixtures, chocolates design, etc. Everything containing cocoa butter and chocolate requires the use of fat-soluble colorings.

🔺 Liquid / gel fat-soluble colorings are represented by a very poor color range. Therefore, compatible colorings were created (when mixed with a water-soluble coloring, they turn it into a fat-soluble one).

🔺 dry (powder) fat-soluble colorings are added to chocolate in dry form and should be thoroughly mixed or processed with an emulsion blender.

📍 Which coloring is better for chocolate: liquid or powder?

Definitely powder! The liquid will inevitably break the structure of cocoa butter, and you might encounter issues when tempering the mixture.

The powder coloring will not affect the mixture in any way, but the weight of the coloring cannot exceed 0.02% of the weight of the product being colored in order to avoid staining the oral cavity.

### Tip B5-23 — Interchangeability of Glucose, Glucose-Fructose Syrup and Trimoline

**Body:**

#marusya_theory_tips

Almost every day I receive DMs with your requests for a piece of advice on this issue. Let's figure it out in detail❤️

The main component in the carbohydrate composition of glucose syrup is glucose. Glucose-fructose syrup usually contains 50% glucose and 50% fructose. These syrups are almost 100% interchangeable.

The sweetness value of syrups is much lower than that of sugar. Just to compare: sugar has a coefficient of 1, while glucose-fructose syrup has a coefficient of 0.6-0.8 (depending on the amount of water in the composition).

📍 Glucose-fructose syrup is also an artificial substitute for honey. The only difference between honey and glucose-fructose syrup is that honey contains pollen and other substances carried by bees that can cause an allergic reaction.

Trimoline (inverted sugar) has a pasty consistency and looks like candied white honey. Its carbohydrate composition is also represented by glucose and fructose, but the percentage of water is significantly lower than that of glucose-fructose syrup.

Thus, the sweetness value of trimoline is much higher than that of sugar. As I've already mentioned, sugar has a coefficient of 1, while trimoline has a coefficient of 1.2-1.4 (depending on the amount of water in the composition).

Glucose-fructose syrup can not always be substituted for trimoline.

You can safely replace one with another if you are making ganache, frosting or chocolates filling. But if you are making various kinds of caramels, syrups or zephyr, meaning the products exposed to high temperatures during cooking, then the glucose cannot be substituted for trimoline due to its sensitivity to high temperatures - at temperatures above 100 °C it can have an unpleasant burnt smell and taste.

📍 When adding trimoline to baked goods (puffs, croissants, etc.), the baking temperature is usually reduced by 10 °C to avoid excessive browning.

### Tip B5-31 — What You Need to Know About Gelatin (Can Gelatin Be Boiled?)

**Body:**

#marusya_about_gelatin

The question of whether it is possible to boil gelatin provokes a lot of controversy and contradictions: some say that it can be done, others - by no means. Let's look into this issue.

So, first of all, I'd like to say right away that GELATIN CAN BE BOILED! However, not in all cases it is necessary to do this - it all depends on how and what you're using it for.

Let's have a look at a few cases.

1️⃣ High concentration of gelatin in liquid

Let's take a no-bake cheesecake as an example. For its preparation, gelatin is diluted in a small amount of water, and then mixed with cream cheese or whipping cream. The gelatin mass turns out to be thick and viscous, and, accordingly, if heated for a long time and strongly, it:

🔸 may set too quickly
🔸 may clump up

Therefore, with a high concentration of gelatin in the liquid, it is not recommended to heat the mass for longer than it takes the gelatine to dissolve completely.

‼️ATTENTION‼️ If you still have brought the gelatin mass to a boil, do not throw it away - just let it cool down a little.

2️⃣ Low concentration of gelatin in liquid

If you need to jellify the juice or fruit puree, you can safely bring the gelatin to a boil. The jellified mass will turn out to be quite runny, so there's almost no risk of rapid setting and the formation of lumps.

However, do not forget that the liquid will boil down and the concentration of gelatin will increase, so keep the mass boiling for literally 1-2 minutes, no more. Excessive concentration of gelatin can negatively affect the taste, making the jelly too thick, and the gelatin flavor more pronounced.

3️⃣ Making the jelly from raw materials of animal origin

If the jelly is made from fish or meat broth, it must be simmered over low heat so that there are no large bubbles, otherwise it won't be clear. But it is not the gelatin that becomes cloudy, but the broth itself!

You can safely use all of the above rules, but it should be remembered that the strength of gelatin is significantly reduced in an acidic environment (pH < 5) and in the presence of protease enzymes.

### Tip B5-32 — Gelatin: Converting Between Different Strengths (Bloom Conversion Formula)

**Body:**

We've already talked about what gelatin is, its usage, types, as well as forms of gelatin and its strength. The beginning of the article can be found by the hashtag #marusya_about_gelatin

Today I suggest talking about conversion between different strengths of gelatin.

When you're going to prepare any pastry product that contains gelatin, it is important to understand that its consistency and texture depends not only on the QUANTITY of the gelatin added, but also on its STRENGTH.

And if the strength of the gelatin in the recipe is different from the strength of the gelatin you use, there is a very high risk of ending up with an unstable confit, which flows out of the cake, or a too thick cheesecake ...

There are 2 ways of getting a stable, predictable result:

1️⃣ Use gelatin with the same strength as indicated in the recipe
2️⃣ Use any gelatin that you have on hand, converting between different strengths

‼️HERE'S THE FORMULA:

In order to convert between different strengths of gelatin, you need to divide the strength of the gelatin used in the recipe by the strength of your gelatin.

G1÷G2

You will get the coefficient by which you need to multiply the weight of gelatin, indicated in the recipe.

For example, a recipe uses 10 g of gelatin Dr. Oetker with a strength of 220 bloom. And you have gelatin with a strength of 180 bloom.

Calculate the coefficient:

220/180 = 1.22

Then multiply this coefficient by the weight of the gelatin in the recipe: 10 g x 1.22 = 12.2 g

If the strength of your gelatin is greater than in the recipe, you will get a coefficient which is less than one. This means that you will need to use a smaller amount of gelatin.

As you can see, conversion is quite simple to do, and at the same time, it helps to avoid undesirable difficulties when working with the textures of your fillings, cakes and desserts..

### Tip B5-34 — Conversion of Recipes for Any Size Cake Molds

**Body:**

Every day I receive a lot of photo reviews about cheesecakes prepared using my recipes: you comment on their mind-blowing tastes, their beautiful appearance, the balance of flavors and textures....

But as soon as it comes to the slightest change in the recipe, such as using a mold of a different diameter, people often get confused and ask me to recalculate the ingredients to fit their mold.

I always try to answer all the questions you have. But I would really like each of you to understand how this can be done on your own, because in fact, if you know the conversion formula, it will be easy as pie.

‼️SO, HERE'S THE FORMULA:

In order to convert the recipe for another size mold, you need to divide the square of the diameter of the larger mold by the square of the diameter of the smaller mold.

d1²÷d2²

And you will get a number by which we need to multiply or divide all the ingredients that appear in the recipe.

For example, the recipe requires a mold of 18 cm in diameter. But you only have a mold of 20 cm in diameter at hand.

You calculate the coefficient:

20*20 = 400
18*18=324

400/324 = 1.23

Since you have a larger mold than indicated in the recipe, you MULTIPLY the amount of all ingredients by 1.23.

If, on the contrary, the mold in the recipe is larger, and yours is smaller, then you DIVIDE by this coefficient.

As you can see, the conversion is not difficult at all.

### Tip B5-37 — How to Make a Perfect Cheesecake (Myths, Baking, Cooling, Decorating, Storage — Q&A Format)

**Body:**

ℹ️ If you open the oven, the cheesecake will collapse.

Stupidity! The cheesecake shouldn't rise at all. If it rises, it means that the temperature that you set for it is too high.

ℹ️ Never open the oven while the cheesecake is baking.

Also wrong. If the cheesecake is baking at low temperatures, then nothing bad will happen if you open the oven.

ℹ️ On average, any cheesecake should be baked for at least 1.5-2.0 hours.

In fact, the baking time will depend on many factors — the characteristics of your oven, the temperature you set (90-150 °C / 194-302 °F), the height of the cheesecake (4-6 cm), taste (chocolate, caramel, berry or fruit), and also the number of cheesecakes (two cheesecakes in the oven will bake 20-30 minutes longer, for example).

That's why there is only one way out — to check its readiness using a piece of advice from the next paragraph. Usually, a cheesecake with a diameter of 16-18 cm and a height of 5-6 cm is baked for about 100 minutes.

ℹ️ How to check the doneness of a cheesecake?

By touching the cheesecake's top with your finger. It should remain clean, while the middle of the cheese should slightly jiggle, like jelly.

✅ Do not worry if some butter leaks onto the baking tray during baking. You used it to grease the ring, and it's natural that it melts at high temperatures. It doesn't affect the texture and taste of the base at all.

✅ Turn off the oven, slightly open the door and leave the cheesecake to cool down inside for about 30-40 minutes. Then take the cheesecake out of the oven and leave it at room temperature until it cools down completely. After that, put it in the fridge for 6-12 hours.

⚠️ If you put a still warm cheesecake in the fridge, the condensation may form and the base will soak from moisture.

✅ Coat the cheesecake with a variety of fillings and décor only after it has completely cooled down (at least 6 hours).

✔️ regular or whipped ganache
✔️ regular or whipped caramel
✔️ nuts, meringue, creams, namelaka, cremeux, gels, glazes, etc.

The shelf life of any product depends on the way it was made, the products used in it and the storage temperature. It's like with zefir – everyone has their own preferences. First of all, YOU should like the cheesecake! It all depends solely on your taste and nothing else!

### Tip B5-38 — What You Need to Know About Gelatin (What Gelatin Is; Sources; Powdered Gelatin Prep)

### Tip B5-40 — Cake Coating, Common Problems: Where Does the Trapped Air Come From?

**Body:**

❌ not verified, inaccurate recipe
❌ you deviated from the cooking instructions
❌ incorrect assembly of the cake.

Air gets inside the cake, naturally, during the assembly process. When we assemble a cake, especially without a cake ring, we very carefully lower each sponge layer on top of the filling with frosting, trying not to press too much, otherwise we risk squeezing the filling with the frosting out.

But it often happens that not all the air has time to get out and some of it remains inside the cake, looking for a perfect moment to put a pastry chef on edge. Usually, because it tries to get out when the chef is about to give the cake to the client.

A similar problem often happens with sponge cakes, especially if we don't give the assembled cake enough time to rest before applying the coating.

### Tip B5-43 — Cake Coating, Common Problems: Cracked Coating — Causes and Fixes

**Body:**

❌ You deviated from the cooking instructions. It's your fault if...
So, the first problem which we've already discussed was cracked coating.

How can you avoid this?

✅ do not violate the technological process and coat the cake with buttercream no earlier than 10-12 hours after assembly, especially if it's a sponge cake

✅ do not make the buttercream layer too thin, unless it is required by the design

✅ use foolproof recipes in which the precise amount of frosting and soaking syrup sufficient to soak the layers are indicated

The second common problem that many pastry chefs might have faced is bubbles on the coated cake.

What's that, what does it look like, why does it happen and how to deal with it?

It happens that you make a cake, keep it in the fridge, everything seems to be as it should be. You coat it with buttercream, decorate, and are waiting for the customer. And then 15-20 minutes before their arrival, you open the fridge, and, gosh, you see a large "tumor" that has formed on the perfectly smooth surface of the cake, as if something is trying to get out of the filling.

Well, it is. Something is trying to get out, but what?

The first problem is a cracked coating. Why does this happen?

Cracks in the buttercream appear due to the fact that the cake layers have absorbed a lot of moisture from the buttercream, it became too dry and cracked.

The most common causes of cracking are:

📌 coating the cake a few hours after assembly

Remember, the technological process of making cakes requires keeping the cake after assembly for at least 10-12 hours in the fridge before proceeding with the coating.

Why? First, the fillings and the frosting need to stabilize and second, which is more important, the cake needs to mature – the cake layers need to soak with frosting.

I prefer to leave the cake in the fridge overnight after assembly and coat it with buttercream only the next day. Thus, the layers will be perfectly soaked and will no longer absorb moisture from the buttercream.

📌 too thin layer of coating

Even if you left the cake in the fridge overnight and it soaked well, parts of the layers that do not come into direct contact with the frosting inside the cake (sides of the layers and the top layer) can still absorb some moisture from the buttercream.

If the coating is very thin and the layers are not sufficiently soaked, it results in cracks on the coating.

📌 insufficient amount of frosting or soaking syrup in the recipe

Finally, the third option that may cause cracks on coating is lack of frosting or syrup inside the cake. Consequently, you get too dry cake layers which actively absorb moisture from the buttercream.

In this case, even though you kept the cake in the fridge overnight after assembly and covered it with a proper layer of buttercream, cracks may appear on the second day after smoothing.
