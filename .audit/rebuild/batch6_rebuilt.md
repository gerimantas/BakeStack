## Tip B6-02: Cake Coating — Leaking Liquid (Whey), Part 1

**Title:** CAKE COATING. COMMON PROBLEMS

**Body:**
The leaking liquid from the assembled cake. Where does it come from?

In fact, in 98% of cases, the reason is very simple. It's all about whey - the liquid residue of cheese, casein and yoghurt production.

It appears especially if dairy products do not contain thickeners and stabilizers, in the event of a violation of the storage temperature and a number of other factors.

❗️ My first leaking cake was a honey cake. In frosting recipe I replaced the sour cream with Greek yogurt. The whey flooded the entire cake base and part of the fridge. Can you imagine how wet the cake was inside?

Therefore, when asked whether it is possible to use yogurt instead of sour cream for frosting, I say "NO"! ❌

The frosting with the addition of even a small amount of yogurt (Greek or Turkish 10-12%) seems to be absolutely stable at first, but over time it liquefies a lot and becomes almost runny. It might be used in sponge cakes if they are not soaked at all, but for honey cakes this option won't work at all.

❗️ The second cake leaked because of the poor quality sour cream - either the storage conditions or the process of preparation were violated.

The remains of sour cream and frosting having spent some time in the fridge released a huge amount of whey. It's logical that the same thing happened with the frosting in the cake - the sponge layers absorbed most of it, but some of it leaked onto the cake base.

❗️ In the third case, the cream cheese was to blame. I usually use Cremette, which contains thickeners and stabilizers, so it releases a small amount of whey.

But one day, for lack of Cremette, I assembled the cake with another cream cheese, which contained neither thickeners nor stabilizers. Also, most likely, the technology of its preparation was also different. Now I understand why it cost almost half the price of Cremette.

That cream cheese released 3-4 times more whey than Cremette. As a result, in the morning there were several tablespoons of sweet liquid on the cake base.

## Tip B6-04: How to Make a Perfect Cheesecake — Myths, Timing, Doneness, Storage, Decoration

**Title:** HOW TO MAKE A PERFECT CHEESECAKE

**Body:**
ℹ️ If you open the oven, the cheesecake will collapse.

Stupidity! The cheesecake shouldn't rise at all. If it rises, it means that the temperature that you set for it is too high.

ℹ️ Never open the oven while the cheesecake is baking.

Also wrong. If the cheesecake is baking at low temperatures, then nothing bad will happen if you open the oven.

ℹ️ On average, any cheesecake should be baked for at least 1.5-2.0 hours.

In fact, the baking time will depend on many factors — the characteristics of your oven, the temperature you set (90-150 °C / 194-302 °F), the height of the cheesecake (4-6 cm), taste (chocolate, caramel, berry or fruit), and also the number of cheesecakes (two cheesecakes in the oven will bake 20-30 minutes longer, for example).

That's why there is only one way out — to check its readiness using a piece of advice from the next paragraph. Usually, a cheesecake with a diameter of 16-18 cm and a height of 5-6 cm is baked for about 100 minutes.

ℹ️ How to check the doneness of a cheesecake?

By touching the cheesecake's top with your finger. It should remain clean, while the middle of the cheese should slightly jiggle, like jelly.

✅ Do not worry if some butter leaks onto the baking tray during baking. You used it to grease the ring, and it's natural that it melts at high temperatures. It doesn't affect the texture and taste of the base at all.

✅ Turn off the oven, slightly open the door and leave the cheesecake to cool down inside for about 30-40 minutes. Then take the cheesecake out of the oven and leave it at room temperature until it cools down completely. After that, put it in the fridge for 6-12 hours.

⚠️ If you put a still warm cheesecake in the fridge, the condensation may form and the base will soak from moisture.

✅ Coat the cheesecake with a variety of fillings and décor only after it has completely cooled down (at least 6 hours).

Now there are only a few questions left to deal with:

ℹ️ What can be used to decorate the cheesecake?

✔️ fresh berries, fruits
✔️ coulis, confit, berry / fruit sauces, jellies, etc., in short, everything that can be made from fruits / berries and their puree with the addition of a thickener
✔️ regular or whipped ganache
✔️ regular or whipped caramel
✔️ nuts, meringue, creams, namelaka, cremeux, gels, glazes, etc.

ℹ️ How long does a cheesecake last?

The shelf life of any product depends on the way it was made, the products used in it and the storage temperature. That's why the only thing that I can say is that on average, the shelf life of baked cheesecakes is no more than 3-4 days in the fridge. Then, how long you will be able to resist this sweet temptation is up to you.

ℹ️ Can cheesecakes be frozen?

If it is dry and there are no foreign odors in the freezer, you can freeze a cheesecake and store it for not more than 20-30 days.

✅ I don't advise freezing cheesecakes if you are unsure about your freezer, but if you need to freeze a cheesecake without a cake base, make sure to wrap it in plastic wrap.

✅ You can turn any large cheesecake into individual cakes, and vice versa. Just keep in mind that the baking time will change. For individual cheesecakes it's 50-60 minutes maximum at the temperature that is 10-20 °C / 50-68 °F lower than usual.

ℹ️ What texture does a perfect cheesecake have?

It's like with zefir – everyone has their own preferences. First of all, YOU should like the cheesecake! It all depends solely on your taste and nothing else!

## Tip B6-05: What You Need to Know About Gelatin, Part 1 (Types)

**Title:** WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW ABOUT GELATIN

**Body:**
At first glance, the topic is very simple and there is not much to learn about, but, in fact, it's not that easy.

Gelatin (French gélatine, from Latin gelātus "frozen") is a mixture of peptides and proteins produced by partial hydrolysis of collagen extracted from the skin, bones and connective tissues of animals. The collagen (protein) in these tissues is converted to gluten.

Gelatin is used in many areas. If we talk about cooking, then it is used to make jelly, confit, mousse, soufflé, marshmallows and so on.

Thanks to it, the mousse cakes keep their shape and tender creams in eclairs are not runny. Gelatin is also used for the production of ice cream to prevent sugar crystallization and reduce protein coagulation.

Edible gelatin used to be a product of animal origin, but with the development of the technologies there have appeared different variations of gelatin that no one could think about.

Notice, now gelatin can be made using a by-products of:

📌 beef
📌 pork
📌 fish
📌 vegetables ❗️

You will also find halal and kosher gelatin.

🌱 Types of gelatin

There are two types of gelatin – powdered and sheet.

📌 POWDERED

It is always soaked in cold water in a ratio of 1: 5 or, depending on the strength of the gelatin, 1: 6.

Mostly, it's "instant gelatin" – it takes just a few minutes for it to swell. Then it is heated until it is completely dissolved.

It is important that the liquid to which the gelatin is added is warm, so that the gelatin disperses well in it, and does not form a huge lump.

Sometimes it is difficult and irrational to heat up the entire mousse or cream. For example, if it's a mousse made with cold sour cream, then a part of it, no more than 1/3, is taken, slightly heated over a water bath or in a microwave, then the gelatin is added to it, mixed well, and then both mixtures – the warm with gelatin and the cold one – are combined.

## Tip B6-06: Cake Coating — Air Bubbles (Why They Form, the Press Pros/Cons)

**Title:** CAKE COATING. COMMON PROBLEMS

**Body:**
How to avoid air bubbles?

I'm sure you sometimes come across photos of assembled cakes with packs of butter or cream on top.

I hope you've already guessed why this is necessary, but in a few words I'll explain what happens.

Being pressed down by some weight, all the air comes out of the cake, and in this way you let the layers tightly stick together for a more secure transportation of the product.

So let's see whether it is obligatory to put a weight on a cake, or such an idea is completely meaningless?

Let's start with where we started.

What to do if air bubbles appear on the side of the cake over time? Answer: put on a press!

There are some simple rules for placing a cake under the press:

📌 the press does not have to be put on for long - several hours will be quite enough, depending on the thickness of your cake and the weight of the press itself

📌 the press is never put directly on the cake, but always on a thick cake base to evenly distribute pressure over the entire surface

📌 the press should be put only on the cake which is tightly tightened with the acetate film, or, better with a cake ring for assembling.

📌 It's better not to use a press for cakes with very delicate and moist fillings.

➕ Pros of putting a cake under the press:

✔️ no air inside the cake and, as a result, no bubbles on coating
✔️ perfectly flat cake surface, which greatly facilitates the smoothing process
✔️ perfectly even layers in the cut, which guarantees a wow effect when cutting

➖ Cons of putting a cake under the press:

✔️ if you put a too heavy press / leave the press for a long time / put the press on a very delicate filling, then you can end up with a too tamped cake.

## Tip B6-07: Cake Coating — Where Air Bubbles Come From (mechanism)

**Title:** CAKE COATING. COMMON PROBLEMS

**Body:**
Yesterday we discussed the possible reasons why the filling may get out of the cake in the form of bubbles on the coating:

❌ not verified, inaccurate recipe
❌ you deviated from the cooking instructions
❌ incorrect assembly of the cake.

But what if you are 200% confident in your filling and, nevertheless, a tricky bubble still has appeared on your finished cake? Or, let's say, you have pierced the bubble and found that ... there is nothing there! 😳

But it cannot be empty, can it? There should have been something inside that pushed the buttercream and formed the bubble! And if this is not a filling, then, of course, it is ... air!

Let's figure out where this air comes from, what it does inside the cake and why it starts to get out at the most inopportune moment.

Air gets inside the cake, naturally, during the assembly process. When we assemble a cake, especially without a cake ring, we very carefully lower each sponge layer on top of the filling with frosting, trying not to press too much, otherwise we risk squeezing the filling with the frosting out.

It is quite natural that many hollows, filled with air, remain inside the cake. What happens next? In the process of maturing, thanks to the gravity, our cake "shrinks", pushing all the air out.

But it often happens that not all the air has time to get out and some of it remains inside the cake, looking for a perfect moment to put a pastry chef on edge. Usually, because it tries to get out when the chef is about to give the cake to the client.

A similar problem often happens with sponge cakes, especially if we don't give the assembled cake enough time to rest before applying the coating.

## Tip B6-10: Cake Coating — Cracked Coating (Cause 1: Coating Too Soon)

**Title:** (untitled — continues CAKE COATING series)

**Body:**
❌ You deviated from the cooking instructions. It's your fault if...

So, the first problem which we've already discussed was cracked coating.

How can you avoid this?

✅ do not violate the technological process and coat the cake with buttercream no earlier than 10-12 hours after assembly, especially if it's a sponge cake

✅ do not make the buttercream layer too thin, unless it is required by the design

✅ use foolproof recipes in which the precise amount of frosting and soaking syrup sufficient to soak the layers are indicated

The second common problem that many pastry chefs might have faced is bubbles on the coated cake.

What's that, what does it look like, why does it happen and how to deal with it?

It happens that you make a cake, keep it in the fridge, everything seems to be as it should be. You coat it with buttercream, decorate, and are waiting for the customer. And then 15-20 minutes before their arrival, you open the fridge, and, gosh, you see a large "tumor" that has formed on the perfectly smooth surface of the cake, as if something is trying to get out of the filling.

Well, it is. Something is trying to get out, but what?

The first problem is a cracked coating. Why does this happen?

Cracks in the buttercream appear due to the fact that the cake layers have absorbed a lot of moisture from the buttercream, it became too dry and cracked.

The most common causes of cracking are:

📌 coating the cake a few hours after assembly

Remember, the technological process of making cakes requires keeping the cake after assembly for at least 10-12 hours in the fridge before proceeding with the coating.

Why? First, the fillings and the frosting need to stabilize and second, which is more important, the cake needs to mature – the cake layers need to soak with frosting.

I prefer to leave the cake in the fridge overnight after assembly and coat it with buttercream only the next day. Thus, the layers will be perfectly soaked and will no longer absorb moisture from the buttercream.

📌 too thin layer of coating

Even if you left the cake in the fridge overnight and it soaked well, parts of the layers that do not come into direct contact with the frosting inside the cake (sides of the layers and the top layer) can still absorb some moisture from the buttercream.

If the coating is very thin and the layers are not sufficiently soaked, it results in cracks on the coating.

📌 insufficient amount of frosting or soaking syrup in the recipe

Finally, the third option that may cause cracks on coating is lack of frosting or syrup inside the cake. Consequently, you get too dry cake layers which actively absorb moisture from the buttercream.

In this case, even though you kept the cake in the fridge overnight after assembly and covered it with a proper layer of buttercream, cracks may appear on the second day after smoothing.

## Tip B6-11: What You Need to Know About Gelatin, Part 2 (Gelatin Mass, Sheet Gelatin, Bloom Strength, Dosage)

**Title:** WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW ABOUT GELATIN

**Body:**
Judging by the number of questions that I often receive, articles with an analysis of the ingredients are very relevant for many of you.

So here is the continuation about gelatin and I kindly ask you to support it with any comment.

A bloomed mixture of water and powdered gelatin is called a gelatin mass.

This term is often found in the recipes of famous chefs. It's very convenient: you make gelatin mass in advance and put it in the fridge, and when you need it, you take it out, weigh the required amount and use it.

The gelatin mass can be stored for up to a week. We'll talk about it in more detail a little later.

📌 SHEET

The gelatin sheets are soaked in a fairly large amount of very cold water – so that the sheets are completely covered with water.

This type of gelatin will absorb just as much water as it needs. Usually it takes about 5-10 minutes.

Before use, squeeze the excess liquid and add the gelatin to the hot mass that you want to gell.

🌱 Gelatin strength

The strength of gelatin is measured according to 2 main classifications:

📌 in Bloom (in honor of Oscar Bloom). The most common gelatin is 125-265 Bloom.

📌 in categories - silver, gold, bronze and platinum (this system is used in America and Australia).

Bronze - 125-135 Bloom
Silver - 160-170 Bloom
Gold - 190-220 Bloom
Platinum - 235-265 Bloom

As for calculating the amount of gelatin, this is an ambiguous question, because it all depends on the density and properties of the mass that is being gellyfied (chocolate in the contents or juice), on the effect you want to get – thick, slightly jiggling jelly or delicate mousse, and on the strength of the gelatin itself.

But, on average, it is assumed that for 500 g of liquid, 10 g of gelatin with a strength of 180-200 Bloom is used.

And then you should decide whether the gelatin is enough, the product is too tender or, on the contrary, thick. Draw conclusions, make adjustments and adapt to the properties of your products.

## Tip B6-12: Cream Cheese vs Mascarpone — What's the Difference

**Title:** LET'S FIND OUT MORE ABOUT CREAM CHEESES

**Body:**
I often receive questions about replacing Philadelphia with Mascarpone in recipes, difference between them and the reasons the frosting suddenly breaks after adding cream cheese to it.

In order to achieve a harmonious combination of ingredients, and sometimes even for the recipe to work, it is extremely important to add the cheese specified in the recipe. Soft cheeses are not always interchangeable!

Let's see what difference is between cheeses and why the interchange doesn't always work.

Cream cheese is classified as fresh cheese. Depending on the technology, there are acid, acid and heat, and acid and enzyme coagulated cheeses.

The most common fresh cheese is cream cheese.

An important feature of classic cream cheeses is their high fat content, which is 60-80%, as well as a pronounced characteristic creamy taste.

📌 What is the difference between Mascarpone and cream cheese?

The fat content of Mascarpone reaches 80%, the taste of the product is slightly sweet and resembles thick sour cream or butter.

❗ Classic Italian Mascarpone, in fact, is not cheese, since special enzymes and lactic acid bacteria are not used to make Mascarpone. Therefore, the name "cheese" is nominal.

📌 How is Mascarpone different from Philadelphia?

✔ Initially, Mascarpone was made in Italy. And it appeared much earlier. Philadelphia, in its turn, was made in the USA.

✔ The fat content of Mascarpone is up to 80%. In Philadelphia it's up to 69%.

✔ Vinegar, tartaric acid or lemon juice are used to make Mascarpone. To make Philadelphia traditional ferments are used.

Before proceeding to the analysis of the interchangeability of soft fresh cheeses Cream Cheese and Mascarpone, let's have a closer look at the process of their manufacturing.

✔ Cream cheese (Philadelphia) is made from two types of milk: skim and pasteurized. In order to denature the protein, a special ferment and heavy cream are added. As a result, this soft fresh cheese acquires a delicate and creamy aroma.

✔ Mascarpone has one of the highest fat contents among soft fresh cheeses. For its manufacture, pasteurized buffalo milk or heavy cream is used, to which the required amount of tartaric acid and white wine vinegar is added. After that, the process of fermentation and coagulation begins. The last stage is cooling. After that, the cheese is packed in tight bags and hung to remove the excess whey.

As you can see, the raw materials for making these two cheeses (skim and pasteurized milk vs heavy cream or buffalo milk), as well as the technologies (ferment vs tartaric acid and vinegar) are completely different.

❗ That's why let me express this thought one more time - Mascarpone and Cream Cheese are not the same !!! ❌

Now let's see if they are interchangeable. Well, to some extent yes, and to some extent - no. How is it possible?

Let's say, if it is really important for you to keep the texture and the taste of the frosting originally suggested by the author of the recipe, then these cheeses cannot be called interchangeable at all.

In fact, Mascarpone cannot be replaced by anything without affecting the taste of the product, as it has a unique viscous and creamy texture.

But what if keeping certain texture and taste of the frosting becomes a low priority, since now you have only Mascarpone / Cream Cheese at hand and you need to assemble the cake right away?

## Tip B6-16: What You Need to Know About Ganache, Part 1 (Definition, Types, Ratios, Basic Method)

**Title:** WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW ABOUT GANACHE

**Body:**
Today is Monday and I am here with a useful post. I'm starting to think about making Monday and Thursday, for example, useful information days. What do you think?

Let's start with the elementary. What is ganache?

I am sure, who is not closely connected with the confectionery world, does not even have the slightest idea what we are talking about. For example, all my friends, every time I say "ganache", are sure that this is some kind of terrible curse 😅

So, ganache is a cream that usually consists of chocolate (white, milk or dark) melted in a liquid (milk, cream, fruit juice, or even crème anglaise).

Ganache can be supplemented on various occasions with sugar, butter or flavors, after which it is cooled and used as intended.

In fact, ganache is a stable and balanced emulsion-like chain in which fat molecules are distributed in an aqueous medium.

There are different types of ganache, depending on its texture:
📌 more and less dense (regulated by changing the amount of the liquid component)
📌 whipped and prepared using Chantilly cream technology (determined by the nature of stirring).

Proportions for a thick ganache suitable for macaron fillings:

📌 On dark chocolate 54.5% - 1 part chocolate to 1 part cream (33%)
📌 On milk chocolate 33% - 3 parts chocolate to 2 parts cream (33%)
📌 On white chocolate 28% - 2 parts chocolate to 1 part cream (33%)

Ganache is used for filling tarts, for classic French cakes, for interlayers in modern cakes, in pastries, in desserts. Ganache is the main filling for chocolate sweets.

It would seem that it's very simple — heat the heavy cream, poured it over the chocolate, punch it with a blender, cool and it's done. But there are difficulties and nuances that you have to know about...

In the continuation of the article, I will tell you the possible causes of stratification, how to properly stabilize and store ganache.

Let's also take a look at all the possible additives and consider how they affect taste, texture and shelf life of ganache.

Today we will figure out how to properly make the ganache using different methods.

📝 Weigh the liquid ingredients (milk / whipping cream / puree / juice), sugar (trimoline, if any) and chocolate exactly by grams.

📝 If you don't use professional chocolate callets (which I highly recommend), you need to prepare the chocolate.

📝 In order to do this, break a chocolate bar into small pieces. The smaller the pieces are, the easier it will be to melt the chocolate.

📍1 Method

📌 Heat the liquid ingredients with the sugar, if it is in the recipe, in the microwave oven or on the stove until very hot and pour over the chocolate. Leave for 20-30 sec and then mix thoroughly with a silicone spatula until homogeneous texture.

📌 If there are small pieces of chocolate in the ganache, you may slightly heat it up in the microwave oven or over the water bath. In no case put it directly on the stove - the mixture will start to burn.

📍2 Method

📌 Pour the liquid ingredients (with or without sugar) into a container with chocolate broken into pieces and heat everything together in the microwave oven several times for 5-15 seconds.

📌 The most important thing here is not to overheat the chocolate! While it is completely hard - check it every 15 seconds - take it out and mix. Once the chocolate starts to melt - every 10 seconds. When only small pieces remain - every 5 seconds. Take it out and mix each time.

📍3 Method*

📌 Heat the liquid part (with sugar, if any) to 85 °C / 185 °F and cool it to 35 °C / 95 °F. While the liquid is cooling, melt the chocolate to 32 °C / 90 °F.

📌 Add the liquid to the chocolate in three steps and mix thoroughly with a silicone spatula each time.

📌 The last step - process the emulsion with a hand blender, trying to prevent the air bubbles from entering the ganache.

📌 If everything was done correctly, the ganache prepared in this way will be stable and ready to be used almost immediately. Also, it will not separate.

## Tip B6-17: Ganache — Broken Ganache Fixes and Storage/Stabilization Tips

**Title:** (untitled — continues ganache series)

**Body:**
🌿 Broken ganache. Possible reasons and way outs:

📌 insufficient amount of liquid - in this case you need to add more whipping cream, milk or puree

📌 poor dispersion of fats in water - process the mass with an emulsion blender

📌 too high indoor temperature – process the mass with a blender and cool it down.

🌿 The obtained ganache can be used immediately - pour it hot into a tart base or onto a sponge, for example. Or you may let it stabilize to the desired texture. The basic principle in the latter case is to prevent the air from entering into it.

🌿 If you want to leave the ganache to stabilize to the desired thickness, it is best to do this at 15-18 °C / 59-64 °F. Pour the ganache onto a baking sheet previously covered with cling film or guitar sheet so that the ganache layer is as thin as possible - this allows the mass to cool faster and more evenly.

🌿 For storage and cooling, you should cover the ganache with cling film or guitar sheet in contact – they should touch the surface of the ganache. Thus, the ganache is protected from air, microorganisms and the evaporation of aromatic substances.

🌿 Transfer the stabilized ganache into a piping bag using a scraper or a spatula. Do not mix or whip it. The texture of the ganache should be stable, but at the same time pliable.

🌿 Store it at 4 °C / 39 °F and use within 2 days. Ganache can be frozen when added to the products in order to streamline the production process.

🌿 It is not advisable to cool the ganache until it is completely stabilized in the fridge. It is better when it sets completely after piping (for example, in macarons).

🌿 If the ganache completely sets, and you start to mix it with a spatula, heat it in a microwave oven, partially knead it in a bag with your hands, then it may result in matte, not beautiful and even broken ganache.

## Tip B6-21: Caramelization and the Maillard Reaction, Part 1 (Caramelization Basics)

**Title:** CARAMELIZATION AND THE MAILLARD REACTION - IS IT THAT EASY TO MAKE CARAMEL? (Part 1)

**Body:**
I feel like writing about something profound. Especially taking into account a recent conversation with one of my curious students. She was wondering why in one caramel recipe the cream is added first, and then comes the butter, while in the other it's vice versa – first we introduce the butter, and then pour in the cream.

So, what's the difference?

There are three large groups of reactions resulting in browned food:
1. Fermentation
2. Caramelization
3. The Maillard reaction

We're only interested in the last two groups, since they take place not only in the process of making caramel, but also in many other culinary processes.

Caramelization is a complex chemical process that takes place when sugar is being heated.

Sugar molecules subjected to high temperatures break down into characteristic aromatic fragments, which in turn form new compounds – polymers. They produce a familiar caramel taste and amber color.

The more complex the sugar molecule, the higher its caramelization point.

For example, for fructose (a monosaccharide) it's 110°C / 230°F, while for sucrose (regular white sugar, a disaccharide) – 160°C / 320°F.

The caramelization point of isomalt is 170°C / 338°F, which allows it to be heated for a long time without changing its color to brown.

There are two ways to caramelize sugar:

🌿 Dry method
- only sugar is used
- requires some practice
- no risk of sugar crystallization during the caramelization process.

🌿 Wet method
- water and sugar are used (1: 2)
- gradual heating of the syrup and evaporation of water facilitates the process of making caramel
- if you make a mistake in cooking, there is a risk of sugar crystallization.

The caramelization issue seems to have been sorted out. No tricks: sugar + high temperature = caramelization.

In the continuation of the article I'll tell you about the Maillard reaction and explain the difference between adding the cream and the butter to the caramel first.

## Tip B6-22: Caramelization and the Maillard Reaction, Part 2 (Maillard Reaction Explained, Answers the Cream/Butter Order Question)

**Title:** (Part 2, continues previous)

**Body:**
Part 2.

Russian version 👉🏻@ma_rusya_manko

The Maillard reaction is a chemical reaction between an amino acid and a reducing sugar, usually requiring the addition of heat.

Excellent example is the crust of roast meat or baked bread which has the typical smell, color and taste of cooked food. These changes are caused by melanoidins - compounds generated in the late stages of the reaction.

It is named after a French physician and chemist Louis Camille Maillard, who was one of the first to study the reaction in the 1910s. There are thousands of melanoidins and other compounds, and about two dozen of them are actively used as flavor additives in food chemistry.

So, the Maillard reaction = [protein (free amino acids) + sugar (monosugars)] + temperature (not necessarily high)

Acidity plays a crucial role in this process. The higher the acidity is, the slower the Maillard reaction occurs.

It means it will never occur when grilling kebabs in a sour marinade. It is for this reason that tomato paste is put into demi-glace sauce only at the last stages, and at the first come bones, meat and vegetables.

Moreover, you should pour the lime juice into the pho broth at the end, but a couple of tablespoons of palm sugar are to be put immediately. So, in no case should you marinate the steak in something sour - you will end up with just marinated meat, not a steak.

Now let's look at the caramel.
The thing is that when we first add 80-100 g of butter (~ 10°C/ 50°F) to 200 g of dry caramel (~ 180 °C / 356 °F), the temperature of the mass quickly drops to ~ 105-110°C / 221-230°F. Then, by pouring in the cream, we will reduce the temperature of the caramel further.

On the contrary, when the hot cream is gradually poured in, the temperature decreases moderately and stays in the range of 140-160°C / 284-320°F longer.

Therefore, in addition to caramelization of sugars, the Maillard reaction also takes place - amino acids in the cream react with sugars, which enhances the aroma and provides a darker shade of caramel.

## Tip B6-23: Pectin vs Agar-Agar vs Gelatin — The Difference

**Title:** [P]ECTIN, AGAR-AGAR AND GELATIN. WHAT'S THE DIFFERENCE? (source shows "ECTIN..." — likely an OCR/extraction artifact dropping the leading "P")

**Body:**
Russian version 👉🏻@ma_rusya_manko

Agar-agar is obtained from seaweed, pectin - from apples and citrus fruits, sometimes from sunflower, pumpkin and sugar beet.

This is how they differ from gelatin, a product of animal origin that is made from many different sources of collagen, mostly from cattle bones and hides, pigskin and fish.

To understand which is better - agar-agar, pectin or gelatin, let's see how each of them works.

Pectin gives products a soft, delicate, slightly viscous consistency. It has no color, but it can add a light sourness.

There are many types of pectin: some need a lot of sugar, that's why they are used for making very sweet masses (jam, confiture, pate de fruit), others work in tandem with calcium that allows us to use them in dairy products. There are pectins of fast and slow setting, thermostable, thermo-reversible, etc.

Agar-agar does not have its own taste and calorie content that's why it is highly valued by confectioners. The use of agar allows you to get a stable thick mass, which will hold the given shape even in the heat. This is very important in making jelly, pastila and pate de fruit.

Agar-agar is thermo-reversible - it can be reheated and then cooled again without loss of quality. This is convenient when one portion of the thickener wasn't enough and you need to add thickness to the mass.

What is the difference between agar-agar and gelatin?

📌 The gelling ability of agar-agar is much better than that of the gelatin, that's why in the preparation process you need 3 times less of it.

📌 Agar-agar sets faster (even at room temperature), while gelatin needs a fridge to set, and all the products made with it melt at 40 °C / 104 °F.

📌 Agar-agar can be dissolved in juices, but gelatin does not like fruit acids, it should be dissolved in water only.

## Tip B6-25: Sour Cream — Origins, Types, Quality, and Substitutes (Crème Fraîche Intro)

**Title:** (untitled — continues sour cream/cream series)

**Body:**
This time we will talk about sour cream. The name of this product is familiar to everyone speaking Slavic languages. In European countries, sour cream became widespread only in the XX century.

Among the Slavic peoples, its name sounds almost the same: сметана, смятана, smotana, smantana, smietana. The Germans call this product schmand or schmetten.

Residents of foreign countries often call sour cream "Russian cream". In many countries there is a product very similar to sour cream. It is called crème fraîche. We will talk about the difference between these products and their interchangeability later.

Sour cream is obtained by fermenting regular milk or cream with certain kinds of lactic acid bacteria. This can be done both at home and in the industry.

Depending on the manufacturing method, sour cream is divided into two types:

📌 produced by households (farm-fresh) obtained by natural fermentation of milk

📌 produced by industrial production, made from whole milk or pasteurized cream with the addition of pure streptococcal cultures.

The first type has a thicker consistency and a taste similar to butter. The advantages of the second are the possibility of producing sour cream with different fat content and a longer shelf life.

Based on the percentage of fat, sour cream produced by industrial production can be:
📌 Light (10-14%)
📌 Regular (15-19%)
📌 Classic (20-34%)
📌 High-fat (35-48%)

In the pastry industry sour cream of 30-40% fat is used most often since it can be whipped. Sour cream is used to make sweet unleavened dough, tea-cakes, muffins, cookies, pies, sometimes sponges and, of course, for making creams.

Let's talk about the quality of sour cream and the possibility of substituting it with other products.

Sour cream is a delicate white dairy product with a moderately thick, homogeneous consistency and glossy surface.

Having the smell, typical for pasteurized dairy products, good-quality sour cream has a pronounced, but pure taste of fermented milk with slight sourness.

Too strong acidic taste or sour smell, the presence of grains in sour cream are the indicators of violations in the production technology or storage conditions of the product.

Both farm fresh and industrial sour cream should not contain anything except milk (cream) and ferments, otherwise this product cannot be called sour cream.

In this regard, it is worth paying attention to the ingredients and name of fermented milk products. The absence of the word combination "Sour cream" on the packaging, as a rule, means the presence of vegetable fats among the ingredients of such products.

What can be used to substitute sour cream in frosting?

I can suggest you several options to choose from, and the first one, preferable, is crème fraîche.

Crème fraîche (lit. from French "fresh cream") is a dairy product with the fat content of not more than 30% (usually 15—18 or 28%).

It is made by fermenting pasteurized cream from cow's milk. Crème fraîche has a pleasant, slightly sour taste, and without additives it is indistinguishable from sour cream either in taste or in the production technology.

In England, Spain, Italy, Denmark, the Benelux countries, crème fraîche and sour creame often stand on the shelves of shops nearby and even have the same manufacturers. Moreover, both names are often present on the same package.

## Tip B6-26: Homemade Cream — Can You Use It? (Heat Treatment Categories, Shelf Life, Fat Content Risk)

**Title:** (untitled — continues cream series)

**Body:**
Can we work with homemade cream? I can't categorically say "no", but I don't recommend working with it either. Why? Let's figure it out.

Firstly, in addition to different fat content, the cream can also be divided into categories according to the method of heat treatment:

📌 Raw – natural.
📌 Pasteurized - heated up to 80 ± 2 °C / 176 °F for 20 sec and then cooled to 4 ± 2 °C / 39 °F.
📌 Sterilized - heated up to 137 ± 2 °C / 279 °F for 4 sec and then cooled to 20 °C / 68 °F.

I think it is obvious to everyone that homemade cream is sold completely fresh, not subjected to any heat treatment, that significantly reduces its shelf life.

The shelf life of homemade cream is from 12 hours to several days, depending on its fat content.

That's why a cake made with fresh homemade cream should be eaten not later than the next day after assembling.

Secondly, most often the fat content of homemade cream which can be purchased on the market is about 50-60%. It's not very convenient, but you can work with it if you dilute the cream with whole milk.

But this is where another problem arises. Since you do not know the exact fat content of your homemade cream, you might make a mistake when diluting it with milk - make it too runny or leave it too fatty.

Let's draw the conclusions. It is definitely possible to use fresh homemade cream for making frosting for a cake, but only for a cake that is meant for your own home consumption. I do not recommend working with it, especially if you bake cakes to order.

## Tip B6-27: Chilling Whipping Cream Before Whipping, and Checking Whipping Cream Quality at Home

**Title:** (untitled — three short Q&A items, continues cream series)

**Body:**
📍 Why should we chill the whipping cream before whipping?

The answer is super simple – by doing this we let the fats crystallize. Otherwise, the whipping cream will quickly collapse or won't whip up at all. In addition, it is recommended to cool also all the tools involved in the work with it, i.e. a whisk and a bowl.

I haven't done this for a long time, and I don't advise you to do it. Why?

Nowadays almost any whipping cream in its contents contains various food additives, namely stabilizers, thickeners and often preservatives. Thanks to these additives, it whips well and keeps its shape perfectly without any problems.

📍 How to check the quality of whipping cream at home?

To do this, simply bring the whipping cream to a boil and look at its consistency. If your hot whipping cream becomes runny like milk, everything is fine, you can work with it.

📍 Why does the packaged whipping cream with a normal shelf life sometimes happens to be as thick as homemade fat sour cream?

Well, it happens sometimes. Dishonest suppliers are to blame for this, because they violated the storage conditions of the product and exposed the whipping cream to temperature extremes to which the latter is extremely sensitive.

## Tip B6-29: Frosting Choice — Low-Fat Sour Cream vs Crème Fraîche (Practical Guidance, Draining Sour Cream)

## Tip B6-33: Cheesecake Baking Temperature — Water Bath Explained, Oven Modes

**Title:** (untitled — continues cheesecake series)

**Body:**
ℹ Cheesecake must be baked at low temperatures (not higher than 120 °C / 248 °F) with a water bath.

## Tip B6-38: Water Bath and Cheesecake — Over vs In, and Why Not To Use "In"

**Title:** WATER BATH AND CHEESECAKE

**Body:**
One of the questions that I receive quite often from people who bought the cheesecake recipe concerns the water bath: what's that? Where should I pour the water?

I'm wondering, without reading the post, what are your ideas? Write them in the comments section, please! I will read your answers with pleasure.

Done? Great! Now let's figure out how the cheesecake should be baked.

In fact, you may bake a cheesecake both over a water bath and in a water bath, or even without using water at all. So, what is the water bath in cooking in general and how is it better to bake a cheesecake - over or in the water bath?

💦 A water bath is a method of cooking, in which the liquid is heated without direct contact of the container with fire. This method is used when the required temperature of the mass that is being cooked should not exceed 100 °C / 212 °F.

💦 Cooking over a water bath suggests pouring the water into the lower bowl or container, and placing the product, which will be cooked over a water bath, in the upper one. In this case, the height of the upper bowl should be smaller in order to leave room for boiling water. Moreover, the water in the larger bowl should not boil too much. This construction is usually used for cooking on the stove.

💦 Cooking in a water bath suggests placing a small bowl directly into a larger container, into which the hot water is poured. The level of water should reach the middle of the inner bowl. This construction is often used for baking in the oven.

When baking over the water bath, the cheesecake is placed on a baking sheet / wire rack at the top of the oven, while the second baking sheet or any other container filled with hot water is placed at the bottom.

When baking in the water bath, the cheesecake pan is placed directly into a large container filled with hot water.

I do not recommend baking the cheesecake in the water bath, as there is a high chance of water getting into the mold resulting in a wet base of the cheesecake!
